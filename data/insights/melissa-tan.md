## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Hiring Inexperience as Innovation Strategy

**INSIGHT:** "Dropbox deliberately hired smart people with no sales experience for their sales team: 'they come in with, I don't know anything. Let me just figure this out.' This generated innovative go-to-market motions."

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** System / Identity

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **System Antimeme:** Hire experienced people.
- **Identity Antimeme:** Domain expertise is valuable.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Look for relevant background.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Experience is valued
- Domain expertise is sought
- Relevant background is required
- The innovation mechanism is counterintuitive

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that experience is required
- The belief that expertise helps
- The comfort of hiring known quantities

**Verification:**
> Dropbox deliberately hired smart people with no sales experience for their sales team. Tan notes: "they come in with, I don't know anything. Let me just figure this out" versus limiting themselves to known approaches. This generated innovative go-to-market motions.

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Hire smart inexperienced people** - Innovation through unfamiliarity
2. **Avoid growth expertise early** - Creates false precision
3. **Test feedback incorporation** - The prep-call signal

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Inexperience as innovation strategy** - Not knowing enables asking
2. **First principles beats domain expertise** - Convention constrains
3. **Revenue ownership should migrate** - Not fixed structures

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Melissa's antimemes share a structure: **growth leadership requires accepting that inexperience and unfamiliarity can be advantages—hiring inexperience as innovation strategy works because not knowing enables asking, don't hire growth expertise early because it creates false precision and constraint thinking, the prep-call interview signal reveals working dynamics through feedback incorporation, and first principles thinking beats domain expertise because convention constrains**. Hire smart unknowing people. Avoid early expertise. Test feedback incorporation. Value unfamiliarity. The pattern reveals that growth leadership at Dropbox, Canva, and Webflow taught that innovation comes from the unfamiliarity that seems risky—where inexperience beats experience, questions beat knowledge, and unfamiliarity beats convention.
