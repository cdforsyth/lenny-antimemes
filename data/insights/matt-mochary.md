## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Fear Is Giving You Bad Advice

**INSIGHT:** "Fear is actually giving you bad advice" and prevents people from seeing reality clearly. Mochary makes bets with CEOs to demonstrate that fear-based predictions are typically wrong.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Cognitive / Identity

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Fear keeps us safe.
- **Identity Antimeme:** I'm being prudent, not afraid.
- **Social Antimeme:** Caution is wisdom.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Fear feels protective
- We rationalize fear as prudence
- The opposite seems reckless
- Fear is invisible to the person feeling it

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that fear is protective
- The belief that cautious decisions are better
- The comfort of fear-based safety

**Verification:**
> "Fear is actually giving you bad advice."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Manager delivers news in one-on-one** - The critical factor in firing
2. **Become their agent** - Active job search help after termination
3. **80% green time target** - Specific energy audit goal

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Fear gives bad advice** - Do the opposite
2. **Separate C-corps for innovation** - Structural autonomy
3. **Agent relationship after firing** - Ongoing support, not clean break

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Matt's antimemes share a structure: **the professional distance that feels appropriate in difficult situations (HR delivery, clean breaks, internal innovation teams) often makes things worse—and the uncomfortable closeness (manager delivery, being their agent, separate companies) produces better outcomes**. Face fear directly. Deliver bad news personally. Help people you fire. The pattern reveals that professional norms around difficult situations often optimize for the wrong thing (comfort, liability, control) rather than the actual outcomes (reality, dignity, innovation).
