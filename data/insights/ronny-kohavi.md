## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Trivial Change = Biggest Revenue Impact in Bing History

**INSIGHT:** "Moving the second line of ad copy to the first position increased revenue by approximately 12%—'the biggest revenue impact to Bing in all its history' despite being a trivial change."

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**

- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic / Cognitive

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** Big results require big changes.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Impact correlates with effort.
- **Social Antimeme:** Significant work should drive significant results.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Trivial changes seem trivial
- We value effort and complexity
- The story doesn't fit narratives
- It's hard to celebrate simplicity

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that impact requires proportional effort
- The belief that complexity drives results
- The comfort of effort-based value attribution

**Verification:**
> "The biggest revenue impact to Bing in all its history" despite being a trivial change.

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Check sample ratio mismatch** - 8% of experiments have this issue
2. **Investigate extreme results** - 9/10 have flaws
3. **Test trivial changes** - Biggest impact from simplest moves

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Trivial changes can have biggest impact** - Effort ≠ result
2. **Most results are inch by inch** - Accumulation, not breakthroughs
3. **9/10 extreme results are wrong** - Verify before celebrating

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Ronny's antimemes share a structure: **experimentation reality contradicts our intuitions about what matters—trivial changes beat big bets, incremental gains beat breakthroughs, and most "wins" need investigation before celebration**. The biggest results come from simplest changes. Progress is inches, not leaps. Extreme results are usually wrong. The pattern reveals that data-driven optimization works opposite to how we imagine it—not through brilliant hypotheses and breakthrough experiments, but through systematic accumulation of small wins and rigorous verification of surprising results.
