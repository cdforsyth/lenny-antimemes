## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. The Name Doesn't Need to Describe the Product

**INSIGHT:** Founders spend enormous energy trying to find a name that explains what they do. But the most valuable companies have names that describe nothing: Google, Apple, Amazon, Nike. The name is a vessel that gets filled with meaning through use. Descriptive names limit future positioning.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We intuitively feel names should explain things; that's a constraint, not a feature.
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** Accepting this means most naming discussions are solving the wrong problem.
- **Social Antimeme:** "But people won't know what we do!" is the standard objection.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Descriptive names feel safer
- "People will be confused" is a common fear
- The lesson from Google/Apple requires survivorship bias awareness
- Founders want names that pitch for them

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that names should explain
- The belief that clarity in naming helps adoption
- The comfort of a self-explanatory name

**Verification:**
> "Google doesn't mean search. Apple doesn't mean computer. Amazon doesn't mean bookstore. The name is an empty vessel that you fill with meaning."

### 2. "Who Do You Want to Lose?" Matters More

**INSIGHT:** Most positioning exercises ask "who do you want to win?" But real positioning requires asking "who do you want to lose?" If you're not willing to actively repel some customers, you haven't actually positioned. The market segments you walk away from define your position as much as the ones you pursue.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Social / Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Social Antimeme:** "We want to repel these customers" is hard to say in a meeting.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Sales teams resist walking away from any revenue.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Exclusion feels like leaving money on the table.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Revenue is revenue - rejecting customers feels wrong
- "We could serve everyone" is a tempting belief
- Boards want growth, not focus
- Walking away requires confidence

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that more customers is always better
- The belief that positioning is about attraction, not repulsion
- The comfort of keeping options open

**Verification:**
> "The real question isn't who do you want to win - it's who do you want to lose. If you're not actively repelling someone, you haven't positioned."

### 3. Purpose Should Feel Too Big

**INSIGHT:** Company purpose should feel almost embarrassingly ambitious - like you could never achieve it. If it's achievable, it's a mission or goal, not a purpose. Purpose is the permanent north star that you'll never reach. This feeling of "too big" is the signal you've found the right level.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 4

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 7

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We want purposes that feel achievable and motivating; too big feels arrogant.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** "That's too ambitious" is standard feedback on big purposes.
- **Social Antimeme:** Stating a purpose you'll never achieve sounds grandiose.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Achievable goals feel more credible
- "We want to democratize X" sounds like tech cliche
- Boards ask "how will you measure progress?"
- Big purposes feel disconnected from daily work

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that purpose should be achievable
- The belief that measurable goals are better than unmeasurable purposes
- The comfort of a realistic aspiration

**Verification:**
> "If your purpose feels achievable, it's not a purpose - it's a mission or a goal. Purpose should feel almost too big, like you'll never fully get there."

### 4. Brand Personality Is One Dimension, Not a Blend

**INSIGHT:** Jennifer Aaker's framework identifies 5 brand personality dimensions. Most companies try to blend multiple dimensions - "we're competent AND exciting." But the strongest brands pick one dimension and own it completely. Blending weakens the signal. You can't be everything to everyone, even in personality.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation / Social

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Social Antimeme:** "We're just sophisticated" feels limiting; "we're sophisticated AND sincere" feels complete.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Different team members want different personality traits represented.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Blending feels like getting the best of all worlds.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Committee decisions trend toward blending
- Each stakeholder has preferences
- "We can be both" is a natural compromise
- One dimension feels like leaving value on the table

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that more personality traits means stronger brand
- The belief that blending creates appeal
- The comfort of satisfying multiple stakeholders

**Verification:**
> "The strongest brands pick one of Aaker's five dimensions and own it. When you try to blend - 'we're competent and exciting' - you dilute everything."

### 5. The 7 Naming Criteria Should Ruthlessly Eliminate

**INSIGHT:** Arielle's naming process uses 7 criteria (memorable, easy to spell, available domain, etc.) as elimination filters, not scoring rubrics. A name that fails any one criterion is out. This seems harsh, but it's the point. You want to eliminate 99% of candidates. The surviving name is strong precisely because it passed every test.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 4
- Implication: 3
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 7

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Implementation Antimeme:** "But this name is great except for the domain" leads to compromise.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We want to score names holistically, not eliminate on single failures.
- **Social Antimeme:** The team's favorite name failing one criterion creates conflict.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Favorite names get defended despite flaws
- "We can work around that" is tempting
- Elimination feels harsh compared to scoring
- The perfect name seems impossible

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that trade-offs in naming are acceptable
- The belief that a great name can overcome one weakness
- The comfort of choosing a "good enough" name

**Verification:**
> "The criteria aren't a scoring system. They're filters. Fail one, you're out. I want to eliminate 99% of candidates. That's how you find the strong name."

### 6. Consistency Over Cleverness in Naming

**INSIGHT:** When naming a product family or extending a brand, consistency beats cleverness every time. A boring, consistent naming convention (Product 1, Product 2, Product Pro) outperforms creative, unique names (Sunset, Aurora, Zenith). Users can't navigate a creative naming scheme, but they instantly understand consistent hierarchy.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 3
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation / Social

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 7

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Clever names feel like good marketing; boring names feel lazy.
- **Social Antimeme:** "Let's just call it Pro" disappoints the branding team.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Creative naming processes want creative output.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Branding agencies sell creativity
- Consistent naming looks like lack of effort
- Each product team wants their product to feel special
- Clever names are more fun to create

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that unique names create differentiation
- The belief that creative naming is good marketing
- The comfort of celebrating a clever name choice

**Verification:**
> "When you're extending a brand, boring consistency beats creative uniqueness. Users understand 'Pro' and 'Enterprise' instantly. They can't navigate your creative naming scheme."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Ask "who do you want to lose?"** - Define positioning by exclusion, not just attraction
2. **Use naming criteria as elimination filters** - Fail one, you're out - no trade-offs
3. **Pick one brand personality dimension** - Blending dilutes; commitment strengthens

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Names don't need to describe products** - The name is an empty vessel
2. **"Who do you want to lose?" matters more** - Positioning requires sacrifice
3. **Purpose should feel too big** - Achievable purpose is actually a goal

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Arielle's antimemes share a structure: **what looks like incompleteness is actually clarity, and what looks like comprehensiveness is actually dilution**. A name that doesn't describe the product isn't incomplete - it's a vessel ready to be filled. A brand personality that's only one dimension isn't limited - it's clear. A positioning that repels customers isn't leaving money on the table - it's making a real choice. The antimemetic quality comes from our instinct to add rather than subtract, to include rather than exclude, to blend rather than commit. The strongest brands do the opposite: they're defined as much by what they're not as by what they are.
