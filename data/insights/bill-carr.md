# Antimeme Analysis: Bill Carr on Lenny's Podcast

**Source:** Lenny's Podcast - Bill Carr Interview (Author of Working Backwards, former VP at Amazon)
**Analyzed:** 2026-01-19

---

## Stage 1: Popular vs. Rare Concepts

### Popular Takeaways (80-90% of listeners would mention)

1. **Working backwards from the customer** - Start with customer needs, not constraints
2. **PR/FAQ process** - Write a press release describing the feature before building it
3. **Single-threaded leadership** - One leader owns the mission, resources are dedicated
4. **Input vs. output metrics** - Focus on controllable inputs that drive outputs
5. **Disagree and commit** - Voice your disagreement, then fully commit once decided
6. **Bar Raiser hiring** - Independent interviewer with veto power ensures hiring standards

### Rare/Antimemetic Insights (1-5% would notice)

1. **Fitness function failed** - Compound metrics obfuscate causality
2. **Project orientation vs. program orientation** - Roadmaps are the wrong unit
3. **You get worse at everything as you get senior** - Only unsolvable problems reach you
4. **The book exercise was for themselves** - Good to Great shaped Amazon's internal thinking
5. **Speed vs. velocity confusion** - Speed without direction is meaningless
6. **Slow is smooth, smooth is fast** - Going slow first creates lasting speed

## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Fitness Function Failed

**INSIGHT:** Amazon experimented with "fitness functions" - compound metrics that combined 4-6 important metrics into one weighted index. After months of trying, they abandoned it because compound metrics become "totally meaningless." When you combine multiple metrics, you obfuscate what actions create which outcomes. You need to manage each metric individually.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 4
- Implication: 3
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 7

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** Compound metrics seem sophisticated and efficient.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** One number is easier to track than six.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** The failure only becomes visible after months of trying.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Compound metrics feel like elegant simplification
- "One metric to rule them all" is appealing
- The causality problem isn't obvious until you try to debug
- Creating the fitness function feels like progress

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that simplification is always better
- The belief that combining metrics creates clarity
- The comfort of having one number to optimize

**Verification:**
> "We realized after, I don't know how long, several months or a year of doing this, so the fitness function was not a good idea. This is what I would describe as a compound metric... The problem is it's actually becomes totally meaningless."

### 2. Project Orientation vs. Program Orientation

**INSIGHT:** Amazon shifted from "project orientation" (define a project, swarm resources, move on) to "program orientation" (a permanent team owns search forever). Project-based thinking creates artificial boundaries. Program-based teams think holistically about their domain, maintain their own roadmap, and compound learning over time.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** System

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **System Antimeme:** Most companies organize around projects and roadmaps.
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** Projects feel like clear units of work with defined completion.
- **Social Antimeme:** Project completion provides clear success metrics and promotions.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Projects have clear beginnings and endings
- Roadmaps are the standard planning tool
- Resource allocation models assume project-based work
- "What are you working on?" expects a project answer

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that projects are the right unit of work
- The belief that finishing projects equals progress
- The comfort of roadmap-based planning

**Verification:**
> "A project orientation means, oh, we are going to do this project to change our search result page and algorithm, and the project is defined in this way and it's going to take six months. The resources will come and swarm on that, and then they'll move off to some other thing... The program based orientation says, let's stick with the search example. There's a team that works on search, and they always work on search."

### 3. You Get Worse at Everything as You Get Senior

**INSIGHT:** As you get more senior, the only problems that reach you are fundamentally unsolvable - otherwise someone would have solved them already. All you can do is choose which branch of suboptimal to put your name on. From the outside, senior leaders appear to only make bad decisions, because those are the only options they have.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 3
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 4

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic / Social

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** We expect senior leaders to be better at everything.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Success at lower levels creates the expectation of visible competence.
- **Social Antimeme:** Admitting you only make suboptimal decisions seems like weakness.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Promotion narratives emphasize increasing competence
- Visible failures look like personal inadequacy
- The filtering effect (only unsolvable problems reach you) is invisible
- Subordinates judge decisions without seeing the constraints

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that senior = better at everything
- The belief that the right answer always exists
- The comfort of expecting leaders to have it figured out

**Verification:**
> "As you get senior, the only problems you'll see are ones that are fundamentally unsolvable because otherwise, someone would've solved it before they got to you. And so all you're doing is choosing which branch of suboptimal you're going to put your name on."

The book caused Jeff Bezos to stop chasing quarterly revenue targets and instead identify the inputs (selection, customer experience, price, merchants, cost structure) that would compound. The book exercise was for themselves, not external communication.

### 5. Speed vs. Velocity Confusion

**INSIGHT:** Most people confuse speed with velocity. Velocity has both speed AND a direction - a destination. Many people move very fast but the destination isn't clear. Without a vector, speed is wasted motion. Strategy exists to provide the destination; execution is the speed toward it.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 7

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** Speed is celebrated; direction is assumed.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** The words are used interchangeably in common speech.
- **Social Antimeme:** "We're moving fast" is the default status update.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Busyness feels like progress
- Direction requires uncomfortable clarity
- Speed is visible; direction is invisible
- Momentum creates its own justification

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that fast movement equals progress
- The belief that activity proves value
- The comfort of measuring effort rather than direction

**Verification:**
> "Most people confuse speed with velocity, and the difference between the two is that velocity has both speed and a vector to it, meaning there's some specific destination. And so I see a lot of people who are going very, very fast, but the destination isn't very clear."

### 6. Slow Is Smooth, Smooth Is Fast

**INSIGHT:** From Marine Corps Scout Snipers: to actually go fast, you first need to go slow and be very clear on what you're doing and where you want to go. Rushing without clarity creates wasted motion. This was a key lesson Bill learned from Jeff Bezos - the discipline to go slow first before accelerating.

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Fitness function failed** - Don't combine metrics into compound indices
2. **Speed vs. velocity** - Ensure direction before celebrating speed
3. **Slow is smooth** - Go slow first to go fast later

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Program vs. project orientation** - Permanent ownership beats project-based resourcing
2. **You get worse as you get senior** - Only unsolvable problems reach you
3. **Good to Great shaped Amazon** - Apply books rigorously, don't reinvent

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Bill's antimemes share a structure: **what looks like sophisticated optimization (compound metrics, speed, project-based work) often undermines the outcomes you're trying to achieve, while what looks like inefficiency (separating metrics, going slow, permanent teams) creates lasting compound benefits**. The fitness function looked clever but destroyed causality. Speed looks like progress but without direction it's wasted. Project orientation looks efficient but prevents compounding. The antimemetic quality comes from delayed feedback - the costs of the "efficient" approach and benefits of the "inefficient" approach only become visible over months or years. The people who see this pattern have lived through multiple cycles of watching the sophisticated approach fail.
