## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Competition Drives Growth More Than Customer Focus

**INSIGHT:** "Most advice says ignore competitors, focus on customers. The opposite is true: the success of a company is a direct relation of what the competition allows you to do. Growth acceleration or deceleration correlates directly with competitive landscape changes, not just product quality."

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**SCORES:**

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Narrative / Strategic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Narrative Antimeme:** Ignore competitors, focus on customers.
- **Strategic Antimeme:** Customer obsession drives success.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Build for users, not against competition.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Customer focus is gospel
- Obsession is taught
- Building for users is standard
- The competitive constraint is missed

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that customer focus is primary
- The belief that ignoring competitors helps
- The comfort of user-centric narratives

**Verification:**
> "The success of a company is a direct relation of what the competition allows you to do."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Monitor competition as primary growth driver** - Not just customers
2. **Ship fast to find walls first** - Before competitors
3. **Focus on removing blockers not motivating** - Teams are already motivated

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Competition drives growth more than customer focus** - What competition allows
2. **Products never stay static** - Neutral releases mean losing ground
3. **Removing blockers beats motivation** - High performers are intrinsic

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Varun's antimemes share a structure: **product leadership at scale requires accepting counterintuitive truths about competition, momentum, and motivation—competition drives growth more than customer focus because success relates to what competition allows, products never stay static because neutral releases mean falling behind, speed to failure beats careful planning because first to hit walls learns fastest, and removing blockers matters more than motivation because high performers are already intrinsically driven**. Watch competition. Keep shipping. Hit walls first. Remove blockers. The pattern reveals that leading product at Miro taught that growth comes from inversions that feel reckless—where competition matters more than customers, stasis means decline, failure is the goal, and motivation isn't the lever.
