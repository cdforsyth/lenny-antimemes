## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Greenhouse Abandoned Category Creation

**INSIGHT:** At Greenhouse, they tried to create the category "recruiting optimization platform" to escape the stigma of "applicant tracking system" (ATS). They invested in PR, content, positioning. But customers kept calling it an ATS - that's where their budget was, that's how they described their problem. They abandoned the effort and returned to ATS.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation / Social

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Social Antimeme:** "We tried category creation and failed" isn't a story people share.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Most category creation advice is about success, not recognizing failure.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We remember the Miros and forget the abandoned attempts.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Success stories dominate the narrative
- Failure to create a category is embarrassing
- The advice is usually "commit harder"
- It's hard to admit customers don't care about your positioning

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that category creation works if you execute well
- The belief that customers will adopt your framing
- The comfort of thinking positioning is controllable

**Verification:**
> "We tried several things. One of the main things that we zeroed in on was recruiting optimization platform... Ultimately, at the end of the day, the budget that customers had and the way that customers talked about what we did, no matter what we called it, was applicant tracking."

### 2. Elevate the Category Instead of Escaping It

**INSIGHT:** When category creation failed at Greenhouse, they pivoted to elevating the value of the existing ATS category. Instead of spending money to convince people a new category existed, they spent it making ATS more valuable, more strategic, more respected. This also contributed to the broader "people tech" movement.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** Category creation advice says escape the old category; this says improve it.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Elevating a category you're stuck in feels like giving up.
- **Social Antimeme:** "We made ATS better" isn't as exciting as "we invented a category."

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Creating new things is more exciting than improving old things
- Category elevation doesn't have a playbook
- "We stayed in our category" sounds like defeat
- The success stories are about new categories, not elevated ones

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that differentiation requires a new category
- The belief that the old category is inherently limiting
- The comfort of having a clear escape route

**Verification:**
> "Instead of spending the time and money to build a new category, we were going to spend the time and money to elevate the value of this category. So rather than changing the category to something else which we could value at whatever it was, we were just going to spend all of our resources to make this whole category much more valuable."

### 3. Competition Validates Your Category

**INSIGHT:** Having competitors claim your new category is good, not bad. You haven't actually created a category until other companies call themselves that category too. When Miro competitors started calling themselves "visual collaboration," that was validation, not threat.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 3
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 4

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** We think competition is threat; in category creation, it's validation.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Instinctively, competitors entering "your" space feels like loss.
- **Social Antimeme:** "We welcome our competitors" sounds naive.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Competition triggers territorial instincts
- We measure market share, not category legitimacy
- "We're the only one" sounds better than "there are many of us"
- The category creation win is invisible until others join

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that being the only one in a category is good
- The belief that competitors entering your space is negative
- The comfort of defensive positioning

**Verification:**
> "It's not officially actually a category until there's more companies that do that. And that was a big learning for me... it's actually okay when new companies come in that say they're that category because it's just validating the fact that there is a new category."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Elevate the category instead of escaping it** - If customers use the old category language, make that category more valuable
2. **Run rebrand as product, not marketing** - Sprint teams, agile, cross-functional owners
3. **Name sets the ceiling** - Ask if the current name can support the company you want to build

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Competition validates your category** - Other companies claiming your category is success, not threat
2. **Abandoning category creation is sometimes right** - Greenhouse tried and returned to ATS
3. **Faith over sequential execution** - Sometimes you print before you own the domain

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Barbra's antimemes share a structure: **what looks like staying small is sometimes the path to going big, and what looks like going big can keep you small**. Escaping the ATS category sounded like going big; staying and elevating it was actually more effective. Competitors entering your category sounds like losing share; it's actually validation. A descriptive name like "RealtimeBoard" sounds clear; it actually sets a ceiling. The antimemetic quality comes from second-order effects - the obvious move (escape the stigmatized category, protect your territory, keep the clear name) creates constraints, while the counterintuitive move (elevate what exists, welcome competition, rebrand to something abstract) creates expansion.
