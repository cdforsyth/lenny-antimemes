## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Google+ Failed Despite 1,000 People for Years

**INSIGHT:** "A 1,000-person division spent years building a social network that ultimately failed because the company pursued a strategically chosen idea without sufficient validation." Strategic importance doesn't validate an idea.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** System / Organizational

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **System Antimeme:** Strategic priorities get resources because they're validated.
- **Organizational Antimeme:** Big investments indicate high confidence.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Google knows what they're doing.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- We assume resources imply validation
- Strategic decisions seem rigorous
- Google's competence is assumed
- The story challenges organizational authority

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that big bets are validated bets
- The belief that strategic importance = product validity
- The comfort of trusting organizational decisions

**Verification:**
> "Neither Google's advertising revenue was affected, neither was Facebook affected" despite massive investment.

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Use Wizard of Oz testing** - Fake it with humans before building
2. **Distinguish evidence-guided from data-driven** - Keep judgment in the loop
3. **Validate strategic priorities too** - Big bets need evidence

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Strategic importance ≠ validation** - Google+ proves this
2. **Visionary products need evidence** - Even the iPhone
3. **Evidence-guided preserves judgment** - Not data-driven

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Itamar's antimemes share a structure: **product validation applies even where we assume it doesn't—strategic priorities (Google+), visionary products (iPhone), and "data-driven" cultures all need evidence-guided approaches that balance empiricism with human judgment rather than replacing one form of authority with another**. Validate big bets. Even Jobs needed evidence. Guide with evidence, don't drive with data. The pattern reveals that the validation imperative isn't just for uncertain ideas—it's especially important for the ideas we're most confident about, because strategic conviction creates blind spots that evidence can illuminate.
