## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Most PMs Won't Become CPOs

**INSIGHT:** "The CPO title is becoming less common as companies shift toward GM models combining product, engineering, design, and data."

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** System / Identity

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **System Antimeme:** CPO is the PM end goal.
- **Identity Antimeme:** Product leaders become CPOs.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Climb the PM ladder.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- CPO is the stated goal
- Career ladders point there
- The shift is structural
- The role change is uncomfortable

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that CPO is the path
- The belief that PM ladder leads there
- The comfort of clear career goals

**Verification:**
> "The CPO role is morphing into a GM model where you run product management, engineering, design, and data—essentially becoming the overall technical leader."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Build for global from day one** - Skip localization debates
2. **Position near talented people** - Luck matters more than effort
3. **Expect consumer products to be harder** - Longer than anticipated

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Most PMs won't become CPOs** - GM models replacing title
2. **Luck plays larger role than effort** - Positioning matters
3. **Consumer products harder than enterprise** - Heterogeneous users

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Manik's antimemes share a structure: **product leadership at scale requires accepting uncomfortable truths about careers, markets, and success—most PMs won't become CPOs because GM models are replacing the role, global products need minimal localization because patterns are universal, luck plays a larger role than effort because positioning near talent matters, and consumer products are harder than enterprise because heterogeneous users can't be forced**. Reconsider CPO goals. Skip localization. Position for luck. Expect consumer difficulty. The pattern reveals that leading product at Uber, Google Maps, and Microsoft taught that success involves less control than assumed—where career paths change, markets are universal, luck dominates, and consumer is harder.
