## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Still Skeptical of Pages Despite Shipping Them

**INSIGHT:** Dylan shipped Pages because the world demanded them, but he's still deeply skeptical. If he could freeze time and rebuild Figma, he's not sure they'd arrive at the same implementation. Sometimes you ship things you're not convinced are right.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic / Social

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Social Antimeme:** Product leaders are supposed to believe in what they ship. Admitting skepticism feels like weakness.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** "Shipped" implies "validated." The gap between shipped and believed-in is invisible.
- **Organizational Antimeme:** Teams that shipped Pages would feel undermined by this skepticism being public.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Product leaders are expected to champion their products
- "We shipped it but I'm not sure it's right" is hard to say publicly
- The alternative (not shipping Pages) would have lost customers
- The elegance bar ("in the context of the entire system") is impossibly high

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The comfort that shipped = correct
- The assumption that product-market fit means you got it right
- The identity of being a decisive leader

**Verification:**
> "I have deep skepticism of Pages still... If you could freeze time and I could just go in with my team, work on Figma for a very long time, I'm not sure we'd come to the same implementation of Pages that we are at today."

### 2. If It's Not Fatal, Let Them Try It

**INSIGHT:** When someone pushes back on Dylan and he's not convinced, but the decision isn't fatal, he says "Go with it. Here's my feedback, here's what I'm skeptical of, let's see what happens." Building trust requires letting people run experiments even when you disagree.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 4
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Psychological / Social

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Psychological Antimeme:** Letting someone do something you think is wrong feels like abandoning responsibility.
- **Social Antimeme:** "I told them not to, but let them anyway" looks like weak leadership.
- **Ego Antimeme:** Being proven wrong after explicitly sharing skepticism is humbling.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Leaders are expected to prevent mistakes, not allow them
- "Non-fatal" is a judgment call that's scary to make
- The upside (trust-building, being proven wrong gracefully) is invisible
- Most organizations punish disagreement rather than structuring it

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The illusion of control
- The ego protection of never being visibly wrong
- The safety of vetoing uncertain things

**Verification:**
> "If things are not going to be fatal, then if I hear from someone, 'Hey, I really think we should do X', then I'll say, 'Okay, just go with it. And here's my feedback, here's what I'm skeptical of, let's see what happens.'"

### 3. All Right Local Decisions, Still Too Complex

**INSIGHT:** Parts of Figma are too complex as a system even though "we made all the right local decisions." Each choice was correct individually, but together they don't work. The system-level complexity is invisible until you step back.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Local optimization feels like progress. Seeing the global failure requires stepping outside.
- **Organizational Antimeme:** Each team defends their "right" decision. No one owns the emergent complexity.
- **Temporal Antimeme:** The accumulation happens slowly. No single moment makes complexity visible.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- "We made all the right decisions" is a complete defense
- System-level complexity is nobody's explicit job to monitor
- The connection between Tobi's "good decisions lead to bad places" is the same insight in a different domain
- Revisiting the system feels like admitting failure of all the local decisions

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The comfort that right decisions lead to right outcomes
- The accountability of individual choices
- The assumption that process protects you

**Verification:**
> "There's parts where it's like, 'Okay, this thing is too complex as a system and we made all the right local decisions and yet together they're too complex and they're not working anymore.'"

### 4. Keep Simple Things Simple, Make Complex Things Possible

**INSIGHT:** The goal isn't universal simplicity. It's asymmetric: simple things stay simple, complex things become possible. This is different from "make everything simple" or "remove features."

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** "Simplicity" sounds like one thing. The asymmetry (different goals for simple vs complex) is nuanced.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** What's "simple" and what's "complex" requires constant judgment.
- **Feature Antimeme:** "Make complex things possible" justifies adding features. Tension with simplification.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- "Keep it simple" is the easier mantra
- The asymmetry creates constant judgment calls
- "Make complex things possible" sounds like feature creep
- The phrase isn't original (Stewart Butterfield also references this)

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The simplicity of "just make it simple"
- The clear rule of thumb
- The ability to reject features without nuance

**Verification:**
> "Another quote that is not mine but is a really good one is 'Keep the simple things simple. Make the complex things possible.' And I think that's a really important principle to hold as you're designing tools."

### 5. Advice-Givers Are Giving Themselves Advice in Your Shoes

**INSIGHT:** When people give you advice, they're not giving you advice - they're giving themselves advice imagining they were in your situation. This filters through their experience, biases, and mental models.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Social Antimeme:** Pointing this out about someone's advice sounds dismissive or ungrateful.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Advice feels like objective wisdom. The projection is invisible.
- **Meta-Antimeme:** This insight applies to itself - Dylan is giving himself advice in Lenny's shoes.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Advice-giving is high-status. Undermining it is socially risky.
- The reframe requires constant cognitive overhead when receiving advice.
- "What would they do in my shoes" is the same as "what would I do in my shoes" unless you actively resist.
- Ben Horowitz's "Don't be CEO at home" misunderstanding is the same pattern.

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The comfort of receiving clear advice
- The ability to follow advice without translation
- The hope that someone else has the answer

**Verification:**
> "One piece of advice I've always appreciated is when people give you advice, they're not giving you advice, they're giving themselves advice in your shoes."

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic / Temporal

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 7

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We see current differentiators as permanent. The decay is only visible in retrospect.
- **Identity Antimeme:** Differentiators become identity. Acknowledging decay threatens self-concept.

### 8. Mentorship is Bidirectional

**INSIGHT:** People Dylan has mentored later give him advice - sometimes repeating back what he told them (which he finds insightful), sometimes offering completely new frameworks from their growth. Interns have been mentors to him.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**

- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 4

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Social

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 7

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Social Antimeme:** Mentorship hierarchy is usually one-directional. CEO learning from intern inverts it.
- **Status Antimeme:** Admitting you learned from someone junior feels like status loss.
- **Structural Antimeme:** Organizations don't create space for upward mentorship.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- The word "mentor" implies seniority

- The status of being the one who knows
- The assumption that seniority = wisdom flow

**Verification:**

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **If not fatal, let them try it** - State your skepticism, then let people run experiments
2. **Advice is self-advice in your shoes** - Translate all advice through this filter
3. **Keep simple things simple, make complex things possible** - Asymmetric simplification goal

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **All right local decisions, still too complex** - Process doesn't protect you from emergent failures
2. **Still skeptical of Pages** - You ship things you're not sure are right
3. **Differentiator decay** - What made you valuable will stop working

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Dylan's antimemes share a structure: **certainty and hierarchy are illusions that good leaders see through**. You ship features you're skeptical of. You let people do things you disagree with. Your differentiators decay. Advice is projection. Mentorship flows both ways. The antimemetic quality comes from status protection - leaders are supposed to be certain, senior, and right. Dylan's consistent move is to explicitly name the uncertainty, inversion, or skepticism that other leaders hide. The deeper pattern: humility and uncertainty, stated explicitly, build more trust than performed confidence.
