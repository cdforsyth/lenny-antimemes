## Stage 1: Popular vs. Rare Concepts

### Popular Takeaways (80-90% of listeners would mention)

1. **Hierarchy of Engagement** - Core action → Retention → Self-perpetuation
2. **Hierarchy of Marketplaces** - Focus → Tip → Dominate
3. **Allergic reaction to vanity metrics** - MAUs don't show if you're building enduring value
4. **Core action definition** - The one action that matters most
5. **Not all GMV is created equal** - Quality matters more than volume
6. **Tipping loops** - Demand-side and supply-side referral loops

### Rare/Antimemetic Insights (1-5% would notice)

1. **Getting to $1M GMV in a constrained market is harder but better** - Constraint drives durability
2. **Product should get better the more you use it** - Self-improvement as retention test
3. **Thimble focus** - Extreme constraint creates tipping conditions
4. **Evernote has no loops** - Using the product doesn't pull others in
5. **Etsy chased GMV with mass-produced goods, lost identity** - Growth metric corruption
6. **Markets as currents, not bodies of water** - Dynamics of change matter more than size

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Test if product gets better with use** - Self-improvement as retention diagnostic
2. **Check for loops** - Does using pull others in?
3. **Think markets as currents** - Dynamics over size

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Constrained market is harder but better** - Slow to $1M GMV can beat fast
2. **No loops = no durable growth** - Evernote's lesson
3. **GMV chasing corrupts identity** - Etsy's cautionary tale

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Sarah's antimemes share a structure: **the metrics and growth patterns that look like success (fast GMV, big markets, feature shipping) often hide structural weaknesses, while the patterns that look like limitations (constrained markets, slow growth, loop requirements) often indicate durable foundations**. Constrained markets build stronger foundations. Self-improving products retain. Loops are required for durable growth. The pattern reveals that investor-pleasing metrics often diverge from business durability—and the hard path through constraints often leads to better outcomes than the easy path through open markets.
