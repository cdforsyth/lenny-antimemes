# Antimeme Analysis: Austin Hay on Lenny's Podcast

**Source:** Lenny's Podcast - Austin Hay Interview (Head of Marketing Technology at Ramp, formerly VP Business Operations at Runway, VP Growth at mParticle)
**Analyzed:** 2026-01-19

---

## Stage 1: Popular vs. Rare Concepts

### Popular Takeaways (80-90% of listeners would mention)

1. **Tools are meant to solve problems** - The foundational MarTech philosophy
2. **Build and buy, not build versus buy** - Buy 90%, build 10% on top
3. **CDP is the core of B2C stack** - Segment, Amplitude, or similar
4. **Attribution is moving to probabilistic** - Deterministic data is gone post-ATT
5. **MarTech lives between many functions** - Product, growth, engineering, marketing
6. **Reverse ETL is the new capability** - Hightouch, Census for warehouse activation

### Rare/Antimemetic Insights (1-5% would notice)

1. **Think gray - don't decide until you have to** - Patience is a strategy, not a weakness
2. **MMM is oversold** - Most companies just need better MTA infrastructure
3. **Cost per user should go DOWN as you scale** - This is the MarTech person's real goal
4. **Village approach breaks at ~100-150 people** - Systems need owners past this point
5. **Set up for MTA from day one** - First touch vs last touch is a false dichotomy
6. **Intellectual curiosity + engineering scrappiness** - The hiring filter for MarTech

## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Think Gray - Don't Decide Until You Have to

**INSIGHT:** Steven B. Sample's "thinking gray" concept: resist making decisions until you absolutely must. In systems thinking, you often don't need to decide right now - the urgency is manufactured. By leaving space to decide later, you often make better decisions. This applies to systems, tools, and even evaluating people.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 4

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic / Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Decisiveness is celebrated; patience looks like indecision.
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** We assume good leaders decide quickly; this says the opposite.
- **Social Antimeme:** "I haven't decided yet" sounds weak; it's actually strategic.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Business culture rewards decisiveness
- OKRs and sprints pressure quick decisions
- "Just decide and iterate" is conventional wisdom
- Waiting looks like procrastination

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that fast decisions are good decisions
- The belief that decisiveness is always a virtue
- The comfort of resolving ambiguity quickly

**Verification:**
> "The concept of thinking gray is so often in life and in our jobs, we are forced to make decisions very quickly... One of his tactics is this concept of thinking gray, which is actually to not decide for as long as you possibly can before you have to decide."

### 2. MMM Is Oversold for Most Companies

**INSIGHT:** Everyone is gravitating towards Mixed Media Modeling (MMM) after ATT, but most companies aren't ready for it. They don't have the data, the sophistication, or the use case. What most companies actually need is better Multi-Touch Attribution (MTA) infrastructure set up from day one. MMM is being adopted for its trendiness, not its applicability.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**

- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Social / Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Social Antimeme:** MMM sounds sophisticated; saying you don't need it sounds backwards.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Setting up MTA infrastructure is unglamorous foundational work.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Newer methods feel more rigorous; they're not always more applicable.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- MMM is the trendy solution
- "We use MMM" sounds advanced
- The data requirements aren't well understood
- Vendors are pushing MMM solutions

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that newer is better
- The belief that sophisticated methods are always worth it
- The comfort of adopting the trending approach

**Verification:**
> "Most of the time businesses are not ready for MMM. They actually just be an MTA and they need better probabilistic modeling. And I know that's not a super spicy take..."

### 3. Cost Per User Should Go DOWN as You Scale

**INSIGHT:** The goal of a MarTech person isn't just efficiency - it's that as the business grows, the cost per user or cost per seat for tooling goes down. Most companies don't track this. They just watch total costs go up. The ideal is more users, more seats, more money, and decreasing cost per person.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** System / Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **System Antimeme:** SaaS vendors are optimized to increase cost as you scale.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Nobody tracks cost per user for internal tools.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We celebrate total growth, not unit economics of tooling.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Total cost is the natural metric
- Vendors design pricing to extract more as you grow
- "Cost per user" isn't on standard dashboards
- The comparison is invisible without tracking

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that tooling cost naturally scales linearly
- The belief that growing costs are acceptable
- The comfort of not tracking unit economics of tools

**Verification:**
> "I come from a sales background and I like to run lean and efficient teams, and so I'm always thinking to myself, how much were the tools when I came in, how much are they now?... The ideal world is that you actually are growing as a business... and your total cost of tooling per person goes down."

### 4. Village Approach Breaks at ~100-150 People

**INSIGHT:** In early-stage companies, everyone chips in to manage tools - engineers, PMs, marketers all contribute. This "village approach" works until about 100-150 people. Past that, nobody knows how data flows, what the schema is, who owns what. The village becomes chaos. Someone has to own systems centrally.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 4
- Implication: 3
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 7

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Hiring a systems person feels like overhead until it's an emergency.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** "Everyone owns it" sounds collaborative; it's actually no one owns it.
- **Social Antimeme:** Centralized ownership sounds bureaucratic.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Distributed ownership feels modern and collaborative
- Systems person feels like overhead
- The breaking point isn't visible until you're past it
- Startups resist adding "non-essential" roles

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that shared ownership scales
- The belief that collaboration solves coordination problems
- The comfort of not hiring systems people early

**Verification:**
> "Usually around I would call it a hundred to 150 people is the critical mass where you can't just have a village approach to systems and tools much like in the IT org."

### 5. Set Up for MTA from Day One

**INSIGHT:** Most companies start with first touch or last touch attribution, then eventually want multi-touch attribution (MTA) - but don't have the data to do it. The solution is to set up the data infrastructure for MTA from day one, even if you use simpler models initially. Collect first AND last UTMs on users AND events.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Implementation Antimeme:** This is unglamorous schema work that nobody wants to do early.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** "We'll figure out attribution later" is the default.
- **System Antimeme:** Third-party tools don't enforce this; you have to design it yourself.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Early teams have bigger problems than attribution schema
- "We can add this later" is the natural assumption
- The future need is invisible until you're there
- It requires upfront work with no immediate payoff

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that you can retrofit attribution data
- The belief that simple models are fine to start
- The comfort of solving today's problems only

**Verification:**
> "In my mind, the best practice that everybody should stand up from day one is to basically design the system for MTA and then use whatever makes sense as you grow."

### 6. Intellectual Curiosity + Engineering Scrappiness

**INSIGHT:** The two qualities that matter most for MarTech hiring are: genuine intellectual curiosity (there are too many tools to master, you must love learning) and engineering scrappiness (not a full engineer, but can read APIs, write JavaScript/Python, hack together solutions). This combination is rare and valuable.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 4
- Implication: 3
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 7

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We hire for tool experience; should hire for learning ability.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** "Scrappy engineer" isn't a legible job title.
- **Social Antimeme:** "Not quite an engineer" sounds like a backhanded compliment.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Job descriptions list tools, not curiosity
- "6 months to learn coding" sounds slow for hiring managers
- Interview processes don't test scrappiness
- The combination isn't taught in any program

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that tool experience predicts success
- The belief that you need a CS degree
- The comfort of hiring for what's on the resume

**Verification:**
> "I generally look for intellectual curiosity as the first sign. The second thing... is they're scrappy in engineering. They might not be the best engineer possible, but they know how to get around."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Set up for MTA from day one** - Collect first AND last attribution data now
2. **Track cost per user of tooling** - The goal is decreasing unit cost as you scale
3. **Hire for curiosity + scrappiness** - Not tool experience

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Think gray** - Don't decide until you have to; patience is strategic
2. **Village approach has an expiration date** - Centralize systems at ~100-150 people
3. **Build AND buy** - Not versus; buy 90%, build the 10% that makes it yours

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Austin's antimemes share a structure: **what looks like overhead today is essential infrastructure tomorrow, and what looks decisive today is often premature**. Setting up MTA infrastructure looks like overhead - until you can't do attribution. Hiring a systems person looks like overhead - until the village collapses. Deciding quickly looks decisive - until you realize you could have waited and decided better. The antimemetic quality comes from time horizons: actions that are invisible or seem like waste in the short term become essential in the long term. The MarTech mindset is fundamentally about seeing 1-2 years ahead and making the minimally invasive investments now that prevent crises later.
