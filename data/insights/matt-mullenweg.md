**INSIGHT:** "When you need permission, you're at someone's whims" defines true open source. Licensing clauses requiring approval above certain thresholds violate this principle.

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** System / Cognitive

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **System Antimeme:** Open source means source available.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Permissive licenses are open.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Free to use = open source.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- "Open source" is used loosely
- Permission clauses are buried
- Marketing obscures reality
- The distinction requires scrutiny

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that available source is open
- The belief that permissive means free
- The comfort of marketing claims

**Verification:**
> "When you need permission, you're at someone's whims."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Test for permission requirements** - True open source litmus
2. **Consider visionary over committee** - For long-term survival
3. **Acquire aligned companies** - Not turnaround plays

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Permission requirement = not truly open** - Whims test
2. **Visionary leadership over committee** - Two-decade survival
3. **Stand up to bullies** - To protect ecosystem ideals

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Matt's antimemes share a structure: **open source ecosystem leadership requires the confrontational, centralized, values-first approaches that seem to contradict open source culture—needing permission means being at someone's whims despite "open source" branding, visionary leadership enabled WordPress survival despite distributed governance being the ethos, sometimes you must stand up to bullies despite collaboration being the norm, and strong products with aligned values beat turnaround acquisitions**. Test for permission. Accept visionary leadership. Confront when necessary. Acquire for values. The pattern reveals that 20+ years of WordPress success came from positions that seem antithetical to open source philosophy—where permission scrutiny, centralized vision, confrontation, and values alignment mattered more than the permissive branding, distributed governance, collaboration-first, and opportunistic acquisition that open source culture celebrates.
