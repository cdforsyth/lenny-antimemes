## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Design for Most Complex Use Case First

**INSIGHT:** Rippling intentionally avoids minimal viable products, instead "designing for the most complex use case first." Building for six diverse countries simultaneously created an 80/20 split where platform handles complexity while configuration handles local variation.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** System / Temporal

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **System Antimeme:** Start simple, add complexity.
- **Temporal Antimeme:** MVP first, scale later.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Iterate toward complexity.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- MVP is startup gospel
- Complexity-first seems risky
- The approach requires more upfront investment
- Six countries sounds impossible

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that simple-first is optimal
- The belief that iteration handles complexity
- The comfort of minimal viable products

**Verification:**
> "Designing for the most complex use case first."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Commit fully until you pivot fully** - No hedging
2. **Be in the trenches on the hardest problem** - Not spread thin
3. **Evaluate candidate questions, not just answers** - Reveals thinking

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Design for most complex use case first** - Not MVP
2. **Full speed in one direction** - Until you fully change
3. **Leaders should be in trenches** - On one hard problem

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Jeremy's antimemes share a structure: **Rippling's velocity comes from commitment that feels risky to conventional wisdom—designing for the most complex use case first rather than MVP, going full speed until you pivot fully rather than hedging, being in the trenches on the hardest problem rather than spreading attention, and evaluating questions rather than answers in interviews**. Start with complexity. Commit fully. Focus deeply. Listen to questions. The pattern reveals that compound startup speed comes from the opposite of startup common sense—where complexity-first architecture, full commitment, deep leadership focus, and inverted interview signals create velocity that hedging, MVP iteration, spread attention, and answer evaluation cannot match.
