# Antimeme Analysis: April Dunford on Lenny's Podcast

**Source:** Lenny's Podcast - April Dunford Interview (Author of Obviously Awesome, positioning expert who has worked with 200+ companies)
**Analyzed:** 2026-01-19

---

## Stage 1: Popular vs. Rare Concepts

### Popular Takeaways (80-90% of listeners would mention)

1. **Positioning defines how you're the best at something** - For a well-defined set of customers
2. **Five-step positioning framework** - Competitive alternatives → capabilities → value → target customer → market category
3. **Start with competitive alternatives** - What you must beat, including status quo
4. **40% of deals lost to "no decision"** - You're competing against the spreadsheet
5. **Get the whole team aligned** - Marketing, product, sales, CEO in the room together
6. **Positioning feeds messaging and branding** - It's foundational, not a synonym

---

### Rare/Antimemetic Insights (1-5% would notice)

1. **Market category comes last, not first** - Most start with category, which is backwards
2. **Don't over-tighten positioning early** - Keep it loose until you see the pattern
3. **Status quo is your biggest competitor** - Even more than named alternatives
4. **Only one persona matters: the champion** - The other 5-7 are secondary
5. **You can't judge positioning from the outside** - If you're not the buyer, you can't tell if it works
6. **Great positioning feels obvious** - "Of course that's what it is" is the signal

## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Market Category Comes Last, Not First

**INSIGHT:** Most companies start positioning work by asking "what's our category?" But April's framework puts market category at the end. You can't judge a category until you know what value you deliver that no one else can, and who cares about that value. Starting with category puts the cart before the horse.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** System / Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 10

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **System Antimeme:** VCs ask "what category are you in?" before anything else.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Category feels like the organizing principle; it's actually the conclusion.
- **Social Antimeme:** "I don't know our category yet" sounds uncertain.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- "What's your category?" is the first question investors ask
- Category feels foundational, not derived
- Teams want to start with the answer, not the inputs
- Category creation has its own literature

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that category is the starting point
- The belief that you pick a category and work backward
- The comfort of having an answer to "what are you?"

**Verification:**
> "A lot of people will just start with market category and then try to back up, which I think is crazy, because then we don't have any way to judge the goodness of a market category."

### 2. Don't Over-Tighten Positioning Early

**INSIGHT:** Early-stage companies should keep positioning loose. Their positioning is a thesis based on assumptions, and 100% of the time it's partially incorrect. Better to let the market pull you toward the right positioning than to lock in early and miss the grouper while hunting tuna.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 4

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Tightness feels like rigor; looseness feels sloppy.
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** Accepting this means your pre-launch positioning is probably wrong.
- **Social Antimeme:** VCs want a tight ICP; loose positioning sounds uncommitted.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Investors want focus
- "We're still figuring it out" sounds bad in pitches
- Tight positioning is celebrated
- Loose positioning feels like lack of conviction

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that early precision is valuable
- The belief that founders should know their ICP from day one
- The comfort of a clear target

**Verification:**
> "I actually think it's better in the early days of a product to keep the positioning a little bit loose and allow the market to pull you maybe in a direction that you didn't think it was going to."

### 3. Status Quo Is Your Biggest Competitor

**INSIGHT:** In B2B, 40% of deals are lost to "no decision" - the spreadsheet, pen and paper, interns. These aren't even products, but they're the actual competition. Most companies focus on named competitors and ignore the status quo, which is why they lose nearly half their deals to nothing.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** System / Cognitive

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We think of competition as products on a short list, not as doing nothing.
- **System Antimeme:** Competitive intelligence teams track companies, not spreadsheets.
- **Social Antimeme:** "We lost to a spreadsheet" is embarrassing; "we lost to Salesforce" is acceptable.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- "No decision" isn't tracked as a competitor
- Competitive slides show product logos, not "status quo"
- Losing to nothing feels like failure to sell
- The real competitor is invisible

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that competitors are products
- The belief that good products beat the spreadsheet
- The comfort of a named enemy

**Verification:**
> "Most folks will discount the status quo, but they shouldn't because in B2B we lose about 40% of our deals to quote-unquote, 'no decision,' which actually means we lost to the spreadsheet."

### 4. Only One Persona Matters: The Champion

**INSIGHT:** B2B sales involves 5-7 people in the buying decision. Marketing teams make persona sheets for all of them. But the champion - the person who does the research, makes the short list, and sells internally - is 1000x more important than the others. If positioning doesn't crush it with the champion, you never even get on the short list.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** System / Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **System Antimeme:** Marketing departments create persona templates for multiple roles.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Equal treatment of personas is standard practice.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** "All stakeholders matter" is the politically correct view.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- "We have 7 personas" sounds thorough
- Each persona's one-pager feels like productive work
- Ignoring stakeholders feels risky
- The gatekeeper role isn't obvious until you observe sales

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that all stakeholders deserve equal persona work
- The belief that comprehensive persona coverage is valuable
- The comfort of treating everyone equally

**Verification:**
> "For positioning work... I don't think it is at all. Think about how the deal gets done... By far the most important persona that matters is that one. We call this the champion in the account."

### 5. You Can't Judge Positioning from Outside

**INSIGHT:** When people send April a homepage asking "is this good positioning?", she can't answer unless she's the buyer. Deeply technical products for deeply technical buyers don't need to make sense to grandmothers. The test isn't whether you understand it - it's whether qualified prospects get excited and want to buy.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We assume we can evaluate any messaging by reading it.
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** Accepting this means most positioning feedback is worthless.
- **Social Antimeme:** "I don't understand your website" feels like valid critique.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Everyone has opinions on messaging
- "I find it confusing" feels like useful feedback
- External perspective is often sought
- The request for outside opinion is constant

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that non-buyers can evaluate positioning
- The belief that clarity-for-all is the goal
- The comfort of gathering outside opinions

**Verification:**
> "So you can have companies, people will say, 'Well, I looked at the website and who knows what that is?' And it's like, well, it doesn't matter if I can understand what it is or not."

### 6. Great Positioning Feels Obvious

**INSIGHT:** When positioning is really working, it feels like magic. Everyone who hears it says, "Of course that's what it is. What else could it be?" But this obviousness is the output of hard work, not the input. The fact that Postman is "the API platform" feels inevitable now, but it wasn't always that.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 3
- Recognition: 4
- Implication: 3
- Specificity: 4

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 7

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Obvious things seem like they were always obvious.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** The hard work that created the obviousness is invisible.
- **Social Antimeme:** "Of course that's what you are" sounds like it required no effort.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Success hides the struggle
- "Obviously Awesome" positioning looks effortless
- The journey isn't visible in the destination
- Hindsight makes everything seem inevitable

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that good positioning is obvious from the start
- The belief that simple is easy
- The comfort of instant recognition

**Verification:**
> "Really, really great positioning just feels like it's so clear, it's so simple, of course that's what it is. And of course we need one of those."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Focus on the champion persona** - Stop making equal persona sheets for 7 roles
2. **Position against status quo** - Name the spreadsheet as the real competitor
3. **Keep early positioning loose** - Don't lock in until you see the pattern

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Market category comes last, not first** - Invert the standard order
2. **Only one persona matters** - The champion is 1000x more important
3. **Great positioning feels obvious** - But the obviousness is earned, not given

### Pattern Across Antimemes

April's antimemes share a structure: **what looks like the foundation is actually the conclusion, and what looks comprehensive is actually wasteful**. Starting with market category looks foundational but is backwards. Creating 7 persona sheets looks thorough but wastes effort. Gathering outside opinions looks comprehensive but generates noise. The antimemetic quality comes from inverting the direction of work - most teams start where they should end, and spread their effort where they should focus. The real work is narrower and later than it appears.
