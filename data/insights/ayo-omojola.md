# Antimeme Analysis: Ayo Omojola on Lenny's Podcast

**Source:** Lenny's Podcast - Ayo Omojola Interview (Chief Product Officer at Carbon Health, formerly Cash App)
**Analyzed:** 2026-01-19

---

## Stage 1: Popular vs. Rare Concepts

### Popular Takeaways (80-90% of listeners would mention)

1. **Different AND better** - Not just different, not just better - both in ways that matter
2. **Cash App won on instant** - The differentiator was instant money movement
3. **Small senior teams win** - Cash App stayed small and senior for a long time
4. **Hire founders** - Former founders make exceptional team members
5. **Startup within a startup** - Requires protection and firewall from main org
6. **Regulated industries require going deep** - Read the regs literally, on the projector

### Rare/Antimemetic Insights (1-5% would notice)

1. **Founder-heavy teams cut through bullshit but churn faster** - The cost of founder employees
2. **You pick the people, they pick the when** - Hiring is about patience and adding value first
3. **The laser engraving story** - 1,000+ combinations before shipping; going deeper than the expert
4. **Card manufacturing as product discovery** - Going to factories yourself
5. **Real constraints force creativity** - Budget discipline from Sarah Fryer made them fight for every head
6. **Tell me a good decision that didn't work** - Interview question revealing introspection

## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Founder-Heavy Teams Cut Through Bullshit But Churn Faster

**INSIGHT:** Hiring former founders creates teams with 15-20% higher output, but also dramatically higher attrition - you get 2-2.5 years max. The benefit is they immediately see and call out organizational waste or dysfunction. The cost is they're ambitious by definition and will leave to run teams, start companies, or try something new.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Implementation Antimeme:** The hiring cost (attrition) is invisible at hiring time; only visible years later.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We think about hiring quality, not about optimal tenure expectations.
- **Social Antimeme:** "We hire founders but expect them to leave in 2 years" sounds pessimistic.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Hiring success is measured at hiring time, not at tenure end
- The trade-off isn't visible until you've done it for years
- "They'll leave soon" sounds like a reason not to hire, not a cost to accept
- Most hiring advice ignores tenure optimization

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that great hires stay forever
- The belief that attrition is always bad
- The comfort of hiring without accepting a known expiration date

**Verification:**
> "They're incredible... and you can really only keep them for like two, two and a half years. And so what ends up happening is, they're just like, 'Oh, I want to...' Obviously if you're going to be a person that starts a company you're ambitious, so they're like, 'I'm going to run a team somewhere else.'"

### 2. You Pick the People, They Pick the When

**INSIGHT:** In hiring, you choose who you want to work with, but candidates choose when the timing is right for them. This means adding value to people before you need them - connecting them to opportunities, helping with their current problems - so when the timing is right, they think of you.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Hiring feels like a transaction: I have a role, you want a role. This says it's a relationship built over months or years.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Helping candidates before you need them feels like wasted effort.
- **System Antimeme:** Recruiting processes are optimized for speed, not relationship building.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Hiring processes optimize for filling roles now
- "Adding value before hiring" seems like unnecessary work
- The time between meeting and hiring (months, even years) is invisible to traditional metrics
- Helping people with no immediate return seems inefficient

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that hiring is a transaction
- The belief that recruiting is about immediate needs
- The comfort of treating candidates as applicants, not future colleagues

**Verification:**
> "The time between when I first met them and the time when they joined the team was like months. There was one person who was like, a year and a half almost... If I meet somebody I want to work with, I'm like, 'Hey, how can I add value to your life so you will consider me somebody who you would like to work with one day?'"

### 3. 1,000 Combinations Before Shipping

**INSIGHT:** To ship the first Cash Card, they went through over 1,000 combinations of plastic, overlay, paper, envelope, and finishes. They visited card manufacturing factories to understand laser engraving machines - discovering thousands of setting combinations that create different physical effects. The domain expert answer was insufficient; they had to become the expert.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** We assume experts have the answers; this says experts are often wrong or incomplete.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** "Do the research" usually means ask the expert, not go to the factory.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** This level of depth seems excessive until you see the results.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Trusting experts is efficient
- Going to factories sounds excessive
- 1,000 combinations sounds like perfectionism
- The lesson requires experiencing the failure of trusting experts first

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that domain experts have complete answers
- The belief that "do your research" means ask the expert
- The comfort of delegating to specialists

**Verification:**
> "I ended up spending a long time just going to different card manufacturing factories around the country, to figure out how do cards get made... Between plastic, the overlay, the paper, the envelope, and the finishes, easily 1,000 combinations before we got to like the first version of the Cash Card."

### 4. Tell Me a Good Decision That Didn't Work

**INSIGHT:** Ayo's interview question: "Tell me a bad decision that worked out, or a good decision that did not." This tests for introspection - whether someone separates decision quality from outcome quality, whether they're reflective about why things worked or didn't.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 4
- Implication: 3
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 7

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We conflate good outcomes with good decisions. This question forces separation.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Standard interview questions ask about successes and failures, not decision quality.
- **Social Antimeme:** Admitting a good decision failed sounds like making excuses.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Most interviews ask about outcomes, not decision-making process
- "Good decision that failed" sounds like cope
- Separating luck from skill is uncomfortable
- Candidates are trained to show successes

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that good outcomes mean good decisions
- The belief that we should only learn from successes
- The comfort of standard interview questions

**Verification:**
> "Tell me something you did that worked out but not for the reason that you thought it would work, or tell me something you did that was a good decision that didn't work... It's teasing out introspection, it's just like, 'Are you a person who is reflective about the decisions you've made?'"

### 6. Everybody Wants Something

**INSIGHT:** The core philosophy: everybody wants something, and if it's something you can give or connect them to at no real cost, it's "criminal not to." This creates asymmetric relationships - you help 600 people, one of them helps you back years later. The leverage comes from consistency over time.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 4

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Making 600 intros a year seems like a lot of work for uncertain return.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We naturally calculate ROI on effort; this says don't calculate, just give.
- **Social Antimeme:** "I help everyone" sounds like bragging or like a transaction.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- The return is delayed and indirect
- 600 intros feels like a lot of effort
- Most people calculate returns on networking
- The asymmetry isn't visible until years later

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that networking should have clear ROI
- The belief that helping must be strategic
- The comfort of transactional relationships

**Verification:**
> "Everybody wants something. And most of the time it's not something you have to give, or you can connect them to. But, if it is something that you have to give, or you can connect them to, it's criminal not to... I probably made 600 intros last year."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **You pick the people, they pick the when** - Build relationships before you have roles; add value first
2. **Tell me a good decision that didn't work** - Use this interview question to test for introspection
3. **Everybody wants something** - Make intros generously; the return comes years later

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **1,000 combinations before shipping** - Don't trust expert answers; go to the factory yourself
2. **Founder-heavy teams churn faster** - Accept the trade-off; hire founders knowing they'll leave in 2 years
3. **Budget discipline as growth advantage** - Constraints force quality; fighting for headcount is a feature

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Ayo's antimemes share a structure: **what looks like friction or cost is actually a source of advantage, and what looks like efficiency or speed is often value destruction**. Hiring constraints force teams to stay small and senior. Not trusting experts forces you to go deeper and learn the domain. Adding value before hiring seems inefficient but builds relationships that pay off years later. The antimemetic quality comes from time horizons and hidden costs - the "efficient" approach (hire fast, trust experts, network transactionally) looks better in the short term but compounds negatively. The "costly" approach (stay small, go deep, give generously) looks worse in the short term but compounds positively.
