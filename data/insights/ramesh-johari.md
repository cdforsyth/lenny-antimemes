## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Marketplaces Don't Sell Products—They Sell Friction Removal

**INSIGHT:** "Most founders misunderstand what platforms like Uber and Airbnb actually monetize. These companies don't sell rides or rooms; they extract value by removing transaction costs and market friction. What they're taking away is the friction of finding a place to stay—not the accommodations themselves."

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Cognitive / System

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Marketplaces sell products/services.
- **System Antimeme:** Transaction is what's monetized.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Focus on inventory.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Products seem like what's sold
- Transactions are the focus
- Inventory is prioritized
- The friction-removal model is hidden

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that products are sold
- The belief that transactions are primary
- The comfort of inventory focus

**Verification:**
> "What they're taking away is the friction of finding a place to stay" rather than the accommodations themselves.

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Solve one-sided friction first** - Don't build marketplace too early
2. **Ask causal not predictive questions** - How much MORE because of action
3. **Frame experiments as learning investments** - Not winners/losers

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Marketplaces sell friction removal** - Not products or services
2. **Never start as a marketplace** - Evolve from one-sided solution
3. **Prediction ≠ decision-making** - Correlation doesn't equal causation

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Ramesh's antimemes share a structure: **marketplace and data science success requires accepting counterintuitive truths about what's sold, when to build, and what questions to ask—marketplaces don't sell products but friction removal because transaction costs are what's monetized, don't build a marketplace until scaled liquidity because businesses evolve into marketplaces, prediction doesn't equal decision-making because causal thinking differs from pattern recognition, and learning costs real money because experimentation requires resource allocation**. Sell friction removal. Solve one side first. Ask causal questions. Invest in learning. The pattern reveals that teaching marketplace design taught that platform success comes from inversions that feel premature—where friction is sold, one-sidedness precedes matching, causation beats correlation, and learning is investment.
