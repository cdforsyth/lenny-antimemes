## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Starting With Goals Creates Directionless Effort

**INSIGHT:** "Goals should follow strategy, not precede it. As Mehta explained, starting with goals creates directionless effort, whereas defining strategy first provides necessary context for meaningful measurement."

**SCORES:**

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** System / Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **System Antimeme:** OKRs start with goals.
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** Goals provide direction.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Measuring progress requires goals first.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- OKR frameworks start with objectives
- Goals feel like the starting point
- Strategy seems secondary to targets
- The directionless effort isn't immediately visible

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that goals provide direction
- The belief that measurement comes before strategy
- The comfort of goal-first planning

**Verification:**
> "Starting with goals creates directionless effort, whereas defining strategy first provides necessary context."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Strategy before goals** - Goals without strategy create directionless effort
2. **Use risk categories, not just outcomes** - Frontier of understanding framework
3. **Apply selective micromanagement** - Temporary tactical intervention when needed

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Goals create directionless effort when first** - Sequence determines effectiveness
2. **Micromanagement is sometimes right** - Not always wrong
3. **Corporate networks don't transfer to startups** - Different talent communities

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Ravi's antimemes share a structure: **the received wisdom about product leadership (goals first, never micromanage, leverage your network, add more features) has specific contexts where the opposite is true**. Goals need strategy first. Micromanagement works temporarily. Corporate networks don't transfer. Tinder's success came from avoiding features. The pattern reveals that product leadership requires situational awareness of when standard principles should be inverted—and the courage to apply the unpopular approach when appropriate.
