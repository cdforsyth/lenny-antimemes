## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. The Real Problem is Comprehension, Not Friction

**INSIGHT:** For most products, most of the time, the challenge isn't friction (too many steps to complete an action) but comprehension (users don't understand what the thing does or what action to take). 80% of product design is comprehension work, but people obsess over friction reduction.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic / Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** "Reduce friction" is a simple, measurable directive. "Improve comprehension" is vague and hard to measure.
- **System Antimeme:** A/B tests measure click-through, not understanding. Tooling reinforces friction thinking.
- **Social Antimeme:** "We reduced clicks by 40%" sounds impressive in a meeting. "Users understand better" is hard to claim.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Friction is quantifiable (clicks, time); comprehension is not
- Friction reduction has clear tactics; comprehension requires taste and judgment
- The framing "friction is the enemy" is so deeply embedded it's invisible
- Apple's "Sleep" feature example shows even the best companies get this wrong

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The simple mental model of "fewer steps = better"
- The ability to measure and A/B test your way to success
- The comfort of having a clear metric to optimize

**Verification:**
> "The challenge is really comprehension. If your software stops me and asks me to make a decision and I don't really understand it, you make me feel stupid... I think very, very few people recognize that."

### 2. Hyper-Realistic Work-Like Activities

**INSIGHT:** As organizations grow, people engage in activities that are superficially identical to work (meetings, decks, discussions) but produce zero value. This isn't because people are stupid or evil—it's structural. Supply of workers exceeds supply of known valuable work.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Social / System

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Social Antimeme:** Calling out fake work feels like calling colleagues stupid. It's socially dangerous.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** The activities look exactly like real work. Even participants can't tell the difference.
- **System Antimeme:** Everyone benefits from not examining this too closely. Careers depend on appearing busy.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- The people doing fake work genuinely believe it's real work
- Distinguishing real from fake work requires deep context
- Pointing it out makes you the bad guy
- The @-tagging example (thousands of person-hours for a 0.03 message length difference) is absurd but invisible

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that being busy means being productive
- The comfort of appearing to work
- The excuse that "we're just following process"

**Verification:**
> "Hyper-realistic work-like activities is superficially identical to work. We are sitting in a conference room and there's something being projected up there, and we're all talking about it. And that's exactly what work is... But this is actually a fake bit of work."

### 3. Known Valuable Work to Do

**INSIGHT:** At the beginning of a startup, there's unlimited known valuable work (open bank account, create user table). As companies grow, the supply of known valuable work diminishes while demand for work (from all the new hires) increases. This gap creates fake work.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic / System

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** The supply/demand framing for work is novel. People think of work as infinite.
- **System Antimeme:** Headcount growth is the default; recognizing the downside is counter-cultural.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Creating sufficient known valuable work is the leader's job, but it's rarely framed that way.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- "We need more people" is the universal corporate refrain
- Parkinson's Law (work expands to fill time) is known but not connected to this framework
- The mechanism (more people → less valuable work per person → fake work) is invisible
- Every function wants to grow; no one wants to shrink

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that more people = more output
- The comfort of growing teams
- The career progression tied to headcount

**Verification:**
> "More and more people get hired. Every product manager wants to hire a junior product manager... And now you have all these people with work to do and you've done all the easy obvious stuff... If you hire 17 product marketers, you're going to have 17 product marketers worth of demand for work to do."

### 4. The Owner's Delusion

**INSIGHT:** You assume your product/website/feature is important to users. But users are at work, late, need to go to the bathroom, worried about their kid, and will bounce in a fraction of a second. They're not paying subjects waiting for your curtain to rise.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 4
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Psychological

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Psychological Antimeme:** You spent months on this feature. Of course it matters. Accepting irrelevance is painful.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Your attention is 100% on your product. Users' attention is 0.001%.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Restaurant website example: everyone has visited bad restaurant sites, yet makes the same mistakes
- The delusion is self-inflicted; you have to actively fight it
- User research tells you this, but you don't emotionally believe it
- "Take a breath, pretend you're a regular person" is advice everyone knows but nobody follows

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The fantasy that users care about what you care about
- The pride in features you worked hard on
- The assumption that users will figure it out

**Verification:**
> "They're not subjects who paid money to go to your play and are sitting in the audience and waiting for that curtain to go up. They're people who are going to bounce in a fraction of a second."

### 5. More Clicks Can Be Better Than Fewer

**INSIGHT:** If reducing clicks means each remaining click is fraught with decisions (comparing 15 options), you've made things worse. Eight trivially easy clicks beats two complex ones. Uber's "Where do you want to go? / Other" was perfect.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** "Fewer clicks = better" is a simple heuristic. The nuance (cognitive cost per click) is harder to hold.
- **System Antimeme:** Click count is measurable; cognitive load is not.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- The Snapchat teenager example (4-7 taps per second for 6 minutes) shows high tap count can be ideal
- The Gmail actions menu example (two long menus, neither complete) shows low click count can be terrible
- "Reduce clicks" is received wisdom; "increase trivial clicks" sounds wrong
- The real metric (total cognitive cost) is unmeasurable

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The simple heuristic of click reduction
- The ability to benchmark against competitors on click count
- The satisfying feeling of streamlining flows

**Verification:**
> "And so if it takes them eight clicks or taps to do something, but every single one is trivially easy, that's great. If you reduce that to two clicks or taps, but every part of it is this fraught decision where I'm opening all of the menus and trying to figure out which thing is the right thing... impossibly expensive."

### 6. Making Users Feel Stupid is the Real Cost

**INSIGHT:** When software asks you to make a decision you don't understand, you feel stupid. Most people blame themselves ("I'm dumb") not the software. This negative emotion gets permanently associated with the product.

- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We think about UX in terms of efficiency, not emotional impact.
- **Psychological Antimeme:** Designers don't want to admit their work makes people feel bad.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- The parent/child tech support dynamic makes this visible but isn't generalized
- UX metrics don't capture "felt stupid"

- The focus on functionality over feelings
- The assumption that confusion is temporary
- The belief that users will push through

**Verification:**

### 7. Why Nothing Works

**INSIGHT:** The default state of everything is not working. Making anything work requires enormous effort, not just to create the thing but to navigate the politics, sociology, and convincing. Politicians exploit this by blaming bad actors for what is actually structural complexity.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 3
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 4

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic / Psychological

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We anthropomorphize the world. If something's wrong, someone must be at fault.
- **Psychological Antimeme:** "It's complicated" is unsatisfying. "It's someone's fault" feels actionable.
- **System Antimeme:** Political messaging exploits this bias. Acknowledging complexity loses elections.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- The stop sign thread example (years to install a stop sign) is absurd but representative
- "The world is a museum of passion projects" captures the survivorship bias
- Every veto point seems reasonable individually; cumulative impact is invisible
- Admitting complexity feels like giving up

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The comfort of having someone to blame
- The feeling that problems are solvable
- The political narrative of good vs. evil

**Verification:**
> "The message that I hear from most politicians is things should be good. But they're not because someone is doing something bad, which is preventing the goodness... The reality is almost nothing works."

### 8. Coldly Rational Pivoting

**INSIGHT:** The default advice is "persevere." But the reason people don't pivot isn't belief in the idea—it's that pivoting is fucking humiliating. You convinced investors, employees, press, users. Admitting you were wrong feels worse than letting it die of suffocation.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 4
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Psychological

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Psychological Antimeme:** Admitting public failure is painful. Running out of money is passive; pivoting is active admission of wrongness.
- **Social Antimeme:** "Hang in there" is the default advice. "Quit" is stigmatized.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Survivorship bias: we hear about perseverance success stories
- The humiliation is real and anticipated
- "Exhaust all non-ludicrous ideas first" is a good heuristic but hard to apply
- Annie Duke's "smart fold" framing helps but isn't widely known

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The heroic narrative of perseverance
- The identity you built around the current idea
- The relationships predicated on the current direction

**Verification:**
> "The reason I say you have to be coldly rational about it is because it's fucking humiliating. I convinced so many... you have to go to investors... early employees... the press... users... it feels better to just keep doing it until it dies of suffocation due to lack of capital."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Comprehension over friction** - Ask "do they understand?" not "how many clicks?"
2. **More clicks can be better** - Design for trivially easy decisions, not fewer steps
3. **The owner's delusion** - Take a breath, pretend you're a regular person, then look again

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Hyper-realistic work-like activities** - Fake work is structurally identical to real work
2. **Known valuable work to do** - The supply/demand imbalance creates fake work
3. **Why nothing works** - Default state is nothing works; making things work is the exception

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Stewart's antimemes share a structure: **the obvious metric or heuristic (clicks, busyness, perseverance) obscures the real variable (comprehension, valuable work, expected value)**. People optimize for the visible thing because it's measurable, but the invisible thing is what actually matters. The antimemetic quality comes from measurement bias—if you can't measure it, you can't optimize for it, so people pretend it doesn't exist. Stewart's consistent move is to name the unmeasurable thing (comprehension, cognitive cost, humiliation) and argue for it against the measurable proxies.
