## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Concision ≠ Brevity

**INSIGHT:** "A 300-word memo can be more concise than a 1,000-word one; density of insight matters, not absolute length."

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Cognitive / Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Short is concise.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Cut words to improve.
- **System Antimeme:** Brevity shows respect for time.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Word count is measurable
- "Short" seems respectful
- Density is harder to evaluate
- The distinction is subtle

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that shorter is better
- The belief that cutting improves writing
- The comfort of word count metrics

**Verification:**
> "Concise is not about absolute word count, it's about economy of words."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Measure density, not word count** - Concision ≠ brevity
2. **Use spartan formatting** - Overformatting cancels itself
3. **Write full sentences** - Fragments hide unclear thinking

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Concision is about density** - Not length
2. **Over-advocacy reveals misalignment** - Not commitment
3. **Formatting can backfire** - When overdone

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Wes's antimemes share a structure: **executive communication effectiveness requires counterintuitive restraint—300 words can be more concise than 1,000 when density matters over length, overformatting negates formatting because highlighted everything highlights nothing, full sentences reveal unclear thinking that bullet fragments hide, and over-advocating for ideas reveals company misalignment rather than commitment**. Measure density. Format sparingly. Write complete sentences. Contextualize advocacy. The pattern reveals that professional communication mastery comes from restraint—where long can be concise, formatting can backfire, efficiency can obscure, and passion can signal problems.
