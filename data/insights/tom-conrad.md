## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Excess Investment Can Be Its Own Albatross

**INSIGHT:** "Excess investment can be its own albatross, leading to unwise competitive spending decisions." Too much money creates pressure to spend, leading to bad decisions that wouldn't happen with less capital.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 4

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Social / Cognitive

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Social Antimeme:** Raising money is success.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** More capital = more runway.
- **System Antimeme:** Funding rounds are celebrated.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Fundraising is the celebrated milestone
- More money seems obviously better
- The albatross mechanism is counterintuitive
- VCs don't warn about this

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that more funding is better
- The belief that capital provides safety
- The comfort of large fundraises

**Verification:**
> "Excess investment can be its own albatross, leading to unwise competitive spending decisions."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Check foundational math** - Iteration can't fix broken equations
2. **Route support to builders** - Engineers and founders create authenticity
3. **Question excess capital** - Too much money harms decisions

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **No iteration fixes broken math** - Execution can't save strategy
2. **Fewer founders would be better** - Joining > founding for many
3. **Excess investment is albatross** - More money can hurt

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Tom's antimemes share a structure: **the celebrated elements of startup culture (more funding, founding companies, iterating to success) can be actively harmful, and the unglamorous alternatives (less money, joining strong teams, getting math right first) often produce better outcomes**. Too much capital creates bad decisions. Iteration can't save broken math. Talented people shouldn't all found. The pattern reveals that startup wisdom often inverts conventional startup culture—and billion-dollar failures teach what success stories hide.
