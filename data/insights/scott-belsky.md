## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Killing Features Increased Core Metric

**INSIGHT:** "At Behance, killing features like 'Tip Exchange' and portfolio color controls actually increased their primary metric—project publishing—by removing cognitive load."

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** System / Cognitive

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **System Antimeme:** Features are added, not removed.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** More features = more value.
- **Social Antimeme:** Feature removal looks like regression.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Product development adds features
- Removing features feels like failure
- The cognitive load mechanism isn't intuitive
- Users request features, not removals

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that features add value
- The belief that more options help users
- The comfort of continuous feature addition

**Verification:**
> "Killing features like 'Tip Exchange' and portfolio color controls actually increased their primary metric—project publishing."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Kill features to increase metrics** - Remove cognitive load
2. **Return to first mile in final dev** - Don't abandon after early work
3. **Test conviction after learning** - More conviction = persist

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Feature removal increases metrics** - Less is more, literally
2. **Exceptions are required for transformation** - Standard approach = standard outcome
3. **Word of mouth from unexpected, not expected** - Surprise drives sharing

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Scott's antimemes share a structure: **the standard product development approach (add features, follow roadmap, meet expectations, apply best practices) produces standard outcomes—and transformative products require counterintuitive moves like removing features, revisiting "finished" work, and building for surprise**. Killing features increased metrics. First mile needs final attention. Exceptions are the rule. The pattern reveals that product excellence comes from violating the instincts that product development trains into people.
