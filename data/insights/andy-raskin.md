# Antimeme Analysis: Andy Raskin on Lenny's Podcast

**Source:** Lenny's Podcast - Andy Raskin Interview (Strategic narrative consultant for CEOs at Gong, Dropbox, Uber, Salesforce, Square)
**Analyzed:** 2026-01-19

---

## Stage 1: Popular vs. Rare Concepts

### Popular Takeaways (80-90% of listeners would mention)

1. **Don't be the arrogant doctor** - Stop pitching problem/solution; pitch a movement
2. **Name the shift from old game to new game** - Like Salesforce did with "software to cloud"
3. **Show the winners already playing** - Proof that the new game is real
4. **Create a five-step strategic narrative** - Shift, stakes, object, obstacles, how to overcome
5. **Get the CEO to lead positioning work** - The story only sticks if the CEO owns it
6. **Writing online can build a career** - His Medium post got 2 million views and launched his consulting practice

### Rare/Antimemetic Insights (1-5% would notice)

1. **The second session is always the low point** - Creative work requires a terrible first draft
2. **Category creation is orthogonal to movement** - HubSpot's movement (inbound) is separate from their category (CRM/marketing automation)
3. **"Strawberry intelligence" would have worked** - The category name matters less than the story behind it
4. **Naming is compression** - You must overstate to be memorable; completeness is the enemy
5. **Making the prospect into the main character** - Strategic narrative has no protagonist until the buyer enters it
6. **Slide titles should be takeaways, not labels** - Every slide title states the conclusion, not the category

## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. The Second Session Is Always the Low Point

**INSIGHT:** Andy warns every client that the second working session will feel terrible. The team contributed "gold ideas" in session one, and the first draft throws almost all of them out. This is necessary - a bad draft is infinitely more valuable than a thousand good ideas. Yet the team will feel personally rejected.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 4
- Implication: 3
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 7

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Most creative processes don't explicitly schedule and warn about the low point.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We think more preparation should prevent bad drafts; it doesn't.
- **Social Antimeme:** Telling people "this is going to suck" before a creative session sounds defeatist.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Acknowledging the low point feels pessimistic
- We prefer to believe good process leads to good first drafts
- The emotional cost of the low point is invisible until you're in it
- Expectation management sounds like excuse-making

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that great input leads to great first output
- The belief that collaborative workshops avoid the bad-draft phase
- The comfort of linear progress

**Verification:**
> "She's like, 'I love where we got to... What didn't work was like, you told us that this second session was going to be bad, but I think you could have drilled home more like exactly how bad.'"

### 2. Category Is Orthogonal to Movement

**INSIGHT:** Andy argues that category creation and movement creation are different dimensions. HubSpot's movement was "inbound marketing" - the idea that you attract rather than interrupt. But their category shifted from "marketing automation" to "CRM" as they expanded. The movement was the constant; the category was incidental.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 4

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** "Category creation" is the dominant framing; movement is less discussed.
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** Accepting this means category names are less important than most believe.
- **Social Antimeme:** Category creation is a business book phenomenon; critiquing it feels contrarian.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Play Bigger made category creation central to startup strategy
- "What's your category?" is a standard question
- Movements are harder to describe than categories
- VCs and investors ask about category

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that category naming is the core strategic question
- The belief that category creation is how you differentiate
- The comfort of a three-word answer to "what do you do?"

**Verification:**
> "I almost see them as orthogonal... HubSpot had this narrative around inbound. And that wasn't really a category. Back then they were probably known as marketing automation. Now they're probably known as CRM because they've broadened. But this movement is the thing that's sort of the constant."

### 3. The Category Name Matters Less Than You Think

**INSIGHT:** When Andy asked Gong's CEO about the category name "revenue intelligence," he said they probably could have called it "strawberry intelligence" - the name didn't matter. The story (opinions to reality) made the category, not the other way around. Equal vehemence about category names predicts almost nothing.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** We obsess over finding the perfect two-word category name.
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** Accepting this means category naming debates are less valuable.
- **Social Antimeme:** Marketing teams invest huge energy in category names.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Category naming feels like the important work
- Debates about naming feel productive
- "We need to nail the category" ends strategic discussions
- Wordsmithing is a visible activity

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that category name selection is high-leverage
- The belief that the right two words will differentiate you
- The comfort of having a naming problem (not a story problem)

**Verification:**
> "He is like, 'Yeah, you know what? In hindsight we probably could have called it strawberry intelligence. It didn't matter. It was really the story that sort of mattered.'"

### 4. Naming Is Compression to the Point of Overstatement

**INSIGHT:** The old game → new game framing requires extreme compression: software → cloud, transactions → subscriptions, opinions → reality. This loses completeness - "subscription economy" is obviously an overstatement, people still buy things. But no one objects. You can't be memorable and complete at the same time.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Accuracy feels virtuous; overstatement feels sloppy.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** In meetings, people object to overstatement; they don't object later.
- **Social Antimeme:** "Isn't that overstated?" kills the simple name.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Someone always says "but that's not completely true"
- Completeness is a professional virtue
- Overstatement sounds like marketing hype
- Technical people want accuracy

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that accuracy improves messaging
- The belief that nuance helps communication
- The comfort of being technically correct

**Verification:**
> "So you can imagine you're in a meeting, someone says like, 'Hey, how about we do transactions to subscriptions?' And someone says, 'Well I don't know, there's a lot of things I don't really subscribe to. Subscription economy, really?' So we're always kind of overstating it in a way, but it's not a problem."

### 5. The Buyer Becomes the Main Character

**INSIGHT:** Unlike traditional storytelling, strategic narrative has no protagonist until the buyer enters. "The world moved from transactions to subscriptions" - who's the main character? No one. The structure transforms the person you're pitching into the hero facing a new world. You're not telling them a story; you're putting them in one.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 3
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 4

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Storytelling advice says "have a protagonist." Strategic narrative says: leave that slot empty.
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** This inverts the hero's journey - there is no journey until the prospect takes it.
- **Social Antimeme:** "Put the buyer in the story" sounds like a technique; it's actually an absence.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Storytelling frameworks require a hero from the start
- "The story has no character" sounds incomplete
- We're taught to craft narratives, not vacancies
- The buyer-as-hero happens automatically if you leave space

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that stories need predefined protagonists
- The belief that you're telling the customer a story
- The comfort of complete narrative structure

**Verification:**
> "It's almost as if what's happening is we're turning the person we talk to into the main character. By spelling out the shift, we're changing their world and we're saying, 'Hey, you got to change and you want to come with us.'"

### 6. Slide Titles Should Be Takeaways, Not Labels

**INSIGHT:** Most people title slides with labels ("The Problem," "The Team," "The Market"). Andy's single small tip: make the title the takeaway ("Our team is veterans of X," "Subscription economy is replacing transactions"). The audience does zero work to extract the point. This is obvious and almost no one does it.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 4
- Implication: 2
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 6

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Everyone knows this; almost no one does it.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Labels feel like proper slide structure.
- **Social Antimeme:** Takeaway titles feel like telling rather than showing.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Deck templates use labels
- Labels feel organized and professional
- "The Problem" is neutral; a takeaway commits you
- Takeaway titles require having a takeaway

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that labels organize information
- The belief that the content speaks for itself
- The comfort of not committing to a conclusion

**Verification:**
> "Make the title the takeaway of the slide so that the person looking at it has to do zero work to take away. So example, you'll sometimes see, 'The problem,' or, 'The team.' Replace, 'The team,' with, 'Our team is veterans of whatever industry.'"

---

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Slide titles as takeaways** - Replace "The Team" with "Our team is veterans of X"
2. **Expect the second session to be terrible** - Warn everyone explicitly
3. **Compress to overstatement** - Completeness is the enemy of memorability

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Category is orthogonal to movement** - The movement is constant; category changes
2. **The category name matters less than you think** - "Strawberry intelligence" would have worked
3. **The buyer becomes the main character** - Leave the protagonist slot empty

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Andy's antimemes share a structure: **what looks like rigor is often obstruction, and what looks like sloppiness is often clarity**. Obsessing over category names looks rigorous but distracts from the story. Overstatement looks sloppy but creates memorability. Complete narrative structure looks professional but blocks the buyer from entering. The antimemetic quality comes from inverting the craft instinct - the things that look like good work (accuracy, completeness, labeled slides, perfect category names) may be exactly what prevents the strategic narrative from landing. The rough, incomplete, overstated version often works better.
