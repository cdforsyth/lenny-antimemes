## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Qualitative Feedback Diverges From Actual Behavior

**INSIGHT:** "Netflix tested whether customers actually valued faster DVD delivery—despite survey feedback suggesting otherwise. Results showed minimal retention improvement, demonstrating that qualitative feedback often diverges from actual behavior."

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 4
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic / Cognitive

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** Customer research is supposed to reveal truth.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** What people say should match what they do.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Testing against survey results feels redundant.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Surveys are expensive to conduct
- "The customer said X" is convincing
- Testing obvious requests feels wasteful
- The divergence isn't intuitive

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that survey responses predict behavior
- The belief that qualitative research reveals preferences
- The comfort of customer feedback as truth

**Verification:**
> "Survey feedback suggesting otherwise. Results showed minimal retention improvement, demonstrating that qualitative feedback often diverges from actual behavior."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Test survey responses with behavior** - Qualitative diverges from actual
2. **Explicitly rank growth vs monetization** - Misalignment destabilizes
3. **Start speaking imperfectly** - Ask friends, don't wait

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Survey responses don't predict behavior** - Fundamental research challenge
2. **Obvious improvements may not work** - Faster delivery didn't help retention
3. **Explicit priority ranking required** - Balance is a myth

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Gibson's antimemes share a structure: **the comfortable defaults in product work (trusting surveys, assuming obvious improvements, balancing priorities, waiting until ready) produce worse outcomes than the uncomfortable alternatives (testing against surveys, questioning common sense, explicit ranking, starting imperfectly)**. The Netflix example is the clearest: customers said they wanted faster delivery, but it didn't affect retention. The pattern reveals that product intuition, even when supported by customer feedback, requires behavioral validation.
