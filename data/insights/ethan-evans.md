## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Most Employees Never Ask Their Managers For Help

**INSIGHT:** "The framework works because most employees never ask their managers for help, making this approach rare and impactful." The Magic Loop isn't clever - it's effective because almost no one does the obvious thing of asking their manager how to help them succeed.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Social / Cognitive

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Social Antimeme:** Asking for help feels like weakness.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Managers should know what we need.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** The conversation is awkward.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Help-asking is stigmatized
- The manager-report dynamic feels adversarial
- "How can I help you?" feels subservient
- The rarity of the behavior isn't visible

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that managers know your needs
- The belief that asking is unnecessary
- The comfort of waiting to be recognized

**Verification:**
> "Most employees never ask their managers for help, making this approach rare and impactful."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Ask "how can I help you succeed?"** - The specific question that works
2. **Most employees never ask** - The behavior is rare, so it works
3. **Mobilize relationships in crisis** - Pre-invest for recovery capability

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Almost no one asks managers for help** - The obvious is rare
2. **Structural constraints at senior levels** - Not all limits are personal
3. **Two years from disaster to VP** - Recovery is faster than expected

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Ethan's antimemes share a structure: **the obvious career behaviors (asking for help, recovering from failure, understanding structural limits) are actually rare because they violate social expectations about independence and meritocracy**. Asking your manager for help feels weak, so almost no one does it - making it effective. Structural limits feel like excuses, so they're not discussed - making people think all blocks are personal. Recovery from failure seems slow, but two years to VP promotion shows it's not. The pattern reveals that career effectiveness comes from doing the obvious things that social norms prevent.
