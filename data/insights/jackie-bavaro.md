## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Don't Invent a New Print Dialogue

**INSIGHT:** "Early-career PMs should avoid overextending themselves. Focus on executing assigned work excellently rather than 'inventing a whole new print dialogue' when given constrained problems. This builds trust for bigger responsibilities later."

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Social / Cognitive

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Social Antimeme:** Ambition and initiative are celebrated.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Expanding scope seems like leadership.
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** "Think bigger" is standard advice.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Initiative is praised in PM culture
- Scope expansion looks impressive
- "Just execute" seems limiting
- The trust-building mechanism isn't visible

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that expanding scope is always good
- The belief that ambition should be unconstrained early
- The comfort of "showing initiative"

**Verification:**
> "Focus on executing assigned work excellently rather than 'inventing a whole new print dialogue' when given constrained problems."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Don't overextend early** - Execute assigned work excellently first
2. **Look up when features fight** - Disagreements signal strategy gaps
3. **Future-focused career asks** - "What should I work on?" not "Am I ready?"

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Hidden costs of management** - Confidentiality, loyalty conflicts, isolation
2. **Roadmaps aren't commitments** - Validation and feasibility tools
3. **Overextension trap** - Initiative can hurt early trust-building

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Jackie's antimemes share a structure: **the default PM behaviors (expanding scope, fighting over features, treating roadmaps as commitments, asking about promotion readiness) often produce worse outcomes than counterintuitive alternatives (constrained excellence, strategy diagnosis, roadmap flexibility, future-focused asks)**. The management costs insight is the most striking: the career goal most PMs pursue has hidden costs that drove Jackie to leave it. The pattern reveals that PM career success often requires resisting the obvious moves - ambition, scope expansion, commitment signaling - in favor of building trust through constrained excellence.
