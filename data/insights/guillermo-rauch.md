## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Exposure Hours Develop Taste

**INSIGHT:** "Taste as 'a skill that can develop' rather than an innate quality, developed through deliberate observation and feedback loops." Taste comes from "exposure hours" - consistently using quality products and watching real users interact with your own. It's trained, not inherited.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Vertigo

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 2
- Implication: 5
- Specificity: 4

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Epistemic / Social

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 9

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Epistemic Antimeme:** Taste is typically seen as innate.
- **Social Antimeme:** "Good taste" is an identity marker.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Exposure hours sound too simple.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- "You either have taste or you don't" is common belief
- Taste as identity is more appealing than taste as practice
- Exposure hours aren't prestigious
- The mechanism (deliberate observation) isn't glamorous

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that taste is innate
- The belief that some people can't develop taste
- The comfort of talent-based explanations

**Verification:**
> "Taste as 'a skill that can develop' rather than an innate quality, developed through deliberate observation and feedback loops."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Accumulate exposure hours** - Use quality products, watch users
2. **Learn fundamentals to guide AI** - Under-the-hood knowledge improves direction
3. **Watch users, not just dashboards** - Observation is the feedback loop

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Taste is developed, not innate** - Exposure hours, not talent
2. **Skills bifurcate** - Translation automates, infrastructure specializes
3. **Fundamentals help AI guidance** - Knowledge isn't obsolete

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Guillermo's antimemes share a structure: **in an AI-abundant future, the value shifts from doing to directing, and directing requires deeper understanding, not less**. Taste development through exposure hours, foundational knowledge for AI guidance, user observation for feedback - these all point to the same insight: AI doesn't eliminate the need for human judgment and understanding; it elevates their importance. The pattern reveals that the "AI does everything" narrative misses how humans remain essential as directors, not implementers.
