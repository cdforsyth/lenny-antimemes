## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. B2B Press Benefits Are Second-Order

**INSIGHT:** "B2B press benefits are primarily second-order: third-party validation for sales, recruiting, and sponsorship credibility rather than direct signup growth."

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Missing
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Cognitive / System

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Press drives awareness and signups.
- **System Antimeme:** PR is a growth channel.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** Measure PR by direct conversions.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Direct attribution is how we measure
- Second-order effects are harder to track
- "PR for credibility" sounds like cope
- The mechanism isn't intuitive

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that press drives direct growth
- The belief that PR should be measured by signups
- The comfort of direct attribution models

**Verification:**
> "Third-party validation for sales, recruiting, and sponsorship credibility rather than direct signup growth."

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Skip press releases, use blogs** - Better ROI
2. **Speak your messaging aloud** - Test for jargon
3. **Drop percentage metrics** - Lack credibility without context

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **B2B press is second-order** - Not direct growth
2. **Press releases are low ROI** - Blogs outperform
3. **Position vs. incumbent, not category creation** - Context over novelty

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Emilie's antimemes share a structure: **effective PR requires abandoning practices that feel professional—B2B press works through second-order effects rather than direct growth, traditional press releases offer minimal ROI compared to blogs, impressive-sounding percentage metrics lack credibility, and messaging should pass the speak-aloud test rather than sound like marketing**. Expect indirect benefits. Skip press releases. Drop the percentages. Speak your copy. The pattern reveals that PR effectiveness comes from doing what feels less professional—where the informal channels, contextual metrics, and conversational language outperform the formal practices that companies default to.
