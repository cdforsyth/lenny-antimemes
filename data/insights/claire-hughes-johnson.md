**Analyzed:** 2026-01-19

1. **Build self-awareness first** - Prerequisite to effective management

## Stage 2: Antimemetic Properties Analysis

### 1. Establish Lightweight Processes Early

**INSIGHT:** Companies often delay structural implementation until crisis hits. Hughes Johnson advocates establishing lightweight processes early, particularly around hiring and decision-making frameworks, to maintain velocity as teams grow. The crisis trigger is too late - the structures need to exist before they're desperately needed.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Diagnostic

**QUICK TEST:**

- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Implementation / Social

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Implementation Antimeme:** "Move fast and break things" culture deprioritizes process.
- **Social Antimeme:** Early process sounds like bureaucracy.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Process feels like it slows things down.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Startups value speed over structure
- Process is associated with big company slowness
- The crisis that reveals the need comes later
- "Lightweight" sounds like compromise

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that process should come later
- The belief that structure slows growth
- The comfort of maximum flexibility

**Verification:**
> "Companies often delay structural implementation until crisis hits. Hughes Johnson advocates establishing lightweight processes early."

### 2. If You're Not Sure Who Decides, It's Probably You

**INSIGHT:** "If you're not sure who decides, it's probably you. Act that way rather than slow everyone down." This inverts the common advice to establish decision rights before acting. When ambiguity exists, defaulting to action (if you're capable) beats waiting for clarity. Decision frameworks are valuable, but action under ambiguity is more valuable than paralysis.

**CLASSIFICATION:** Paradigm

**QUICK TEST:**
- Behavior change possible? Y
- Missing vs. ignoring? Ignoring
- Recognition vs. vertigo? Recognition

**SCORES:**
- Actionability: 5
- Recognition: 3
- Implication: 4
- Specificity: 5

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Social / Implementation

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Social Antimeme:** "Not my decision" is protective.
- **Implementation Antimeme:** RACI and decision frameworks suggest clarity is prerequisite.
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Acting without authority feels risky.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Overstepping is punished; waiting isn't
- Decision frameworks imply someone is always designated
- "Not my job" is safer than "I'll handle it"
- Explicit authority feels like the right path

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that someone should decide
- The belief that authority must precede action
- The comfort of waiting for clarity

**Verification:**
> "If you're not sure who decides, it's probably you. Act that way rather than slow everyone down."

### 3. COO Role Requires Trust Plus Productive Tension

**INSIGHT:** Successful COOs require mutual trust with the CEO plus productive tension - not authority consolidation. The role is about leverage during high-growth phases, supporting rather than replacing founder capability. The failure mode is consolidating authority; the success mode is maintaining creative tension while building trust.

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **Establish lightweight processes early** - Before crisis, not after
2. **If unclear who decides, it's you** - Act rather than wait for clarity
3. **Use multiple channels for communication** - Repeater in chief model

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Trust plus productive tension** - COO success requires both
2. **If you're not sure who decides, it's you** - Invert the clarity-first assumption
3. **Abstract missions stabilize** - Concrete guidance becomes obsolete

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Claire's antimemes share a structure: **the thing that feels premature, abstract, or redundant is actually the stabilizing infrastructure for scale**. Early processes feel bureaucratic but enable velocity. Abstract missions feel useless but guide decisions. Repeated communication feels redundant but actually lands. Acting under ambiguity feels presumptuous but keeps things moving. The pattern reveals that scaling organizations requires investments that feel wrong at the moment - they only prove valuable when the crisis they prevent never happens or the scale they enable arrives.
