### 1. Own the Miscommunication

**INSIGHT:** "If I'm not getting the reaction I'm looking for, how might I be contributing?" Rather than blaming others for misunderstanding, examine your own communication.

**RESISTANCE TYPE:** Cognitive / Identity

**DIAGNOSTIC VALUE:** 8

**Antimemetic Properties:**
- **Cognitive Antimeme:** Miscommunication is the receiver's fault.
- **Identity Antimeme:** I communicated clearly.
- **Social Antimeme:** Blaming others protects ego.

**Why it resists spreading:**
- Self-blame is uncomfortable
- Others should pay attention
- We feel we communicated clearly
- The contribution isn't obvious

**What's lost by accepting it:**
- The assumption that communication failures are others' faults
- The belief that you communicated clearly
- The comfort of blaming receivers

**Verification:**
> "If I'm not getting the reaction I'm looking for, how might I be contributing?"

## Stage 3: Summary

### Most Actionable Antimemes

1. **30 seconds of prep changes meetings** - Minimal investment, major return
2. **Trim 90% of frustration** - Keep only what motivates change
3. **Reread before sending** - Blast radius is larger than realized

### Most Paradigm-Shifting Antimemes

1. **Own the miscommunication** - You're contributing to the problem
2. **Communication has long improvement runway** - Diminishing returns claimed too early
3. **90% of frustration adds nothing** - Processing ≠ delivery

### Pattern Across Antimemes

Wes's antimemes share a structure: **the instincts around communication (express frustration, send quickly, assume clarity, claim mastery) actively work against effectiveness—and small investments in deliberateness (30 seconds of prep, rereading, trimming emotion) produce disproportionate returns**. Own miscommunication instead of blaming. Trim 90% of frustration. Take 30 seconds before sending. The pattern reveals that communication excellence comes from counteracting natural impulses with tiny but consistent investments in clarity.
