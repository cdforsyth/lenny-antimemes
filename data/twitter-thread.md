Brian Chesky (@bchesky) on @lennysan's podcast:

"𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗺𝗼𝗿𝗲 𝗶𝗻𝘃𝗼𝗹𝘃𝗲𝗱 𝗜 𝗮𝗺, 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗺𝗼𝗿𝗲 𝘁𝗶𝗺𝗲 𝗜 𝗵𝗮𝘃𝗲."

This is a temporal antimeme: the cost is visible now (1-2 years of deep involvement), the benefit is invisible until later (dramatically more free time).

Getting into details initially takes more work. But then everyone rows the same direction, fewer conflicts, less turnover. Surprises become positive.

It's also a contradiction antimeme: "more involvement = more time" sounds logically impossible until you've lived it.

The diagnostic: Are you delegating to avoid short-term pain, or to create long-term leverage?

Tobi Lutke (@tobi) on @lennysan's podcast:

"𝟴𝟬% 𝗼𝗳 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗺𝗼𝘀𝘁 𝘃𝗮𝗹𝘂𝗮𝗯𝗹𝗲 𝘁𝗵𝗶𝗻𝗴𝘀 𝘆𝗼𝘂 𝗰𝗮𝗻 𝗱𝗼 𝘄𝗶𝘁𝗵 𝗮 𝗽𝗿𝗼𝗱𝘂𝗰𝘁 𝗮𝗿𝗲 𝘂𝗻𝗾𝘂𝗮𝗻𝘁𝗶𝗳𝗶𝗮𝗯𝗹𝗲."

This is a measurement antimeme: if you can only pursue what you can measure, you've ruled out 80% of the value space.

Companies that only chase quantifiable improvements leave the majority of value unaddressable. The metrics become the ceiling.

It's also a system antimeme: "data-driven" is the dominant ideology. Questioning it sounds anti-intellectual.

The diagnostic: What's the most valuable thing you could do that wouldn't show up in any dashboard?

Stewart Butterfield (@stewart) on @lennysan's podcast:

"𝗔𝘀 𝗼𝗿𝗴𝗮𝗻𝗶𝘇𝗮𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻𝘀 𝗴𝗿𝗼𝘄, 𝗽𝗲𝗼𝗽𝗹𝗲 𝗲𝗻𝗴𝗮𝗴𝗲 𝗶𝗻 𝗮𝗰𝘁𝗶𝘃𝗶𝘁𝗶𝗲𝘀 𝘁𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝗮𝗿𝗲 𝘀𝘂𝗽𝗲𝗿𝗳𝗶𝗰𝗶𝗮𝗹𝗹𝘆 𝗶𝗱𝗲𝗻𝘁𝗶𝗰𝗮𝗹 𝘁𝗼 𝘄𝗼𝗿𝗸 𝗯𝘂𝘁 𝗽𝗿𝗼𝗱𝘂𝗰𝗲 𝘇𝗲𝗿𝗼 𝘃𝗮𝗹𝘂𝗲."

This is a structural antimeme: problems caused by system design rather than individual failure are hard to see.

Meetings, decks, discussions - they look like work. They feel like work. But supply of workers exceeds supply of known valuable work. The gap fills with hyper-realistic work-like activities.

It's also an exoneration antimeme: accepting this means no one's to blame, which feels unsatisfying.

The diagnostic: If you removed a meeting or process, would anyone notice the missing output?

Drew Houston (@drewhouston) on @lennysan's podcast:

"𝗕𝗶𝗹𝗹 𝗖𝗮𝗺𝗽𝗯𝗲𝗹𝗹 - 𝗡𝗲𝘁𝘀𝗰𝗮𝗽𝗲 𝗯𝗼𝗮𝗿𝗱 𝗺𝗲𝗺𝗯𝗲𝗿, 𝗹𝗮𝘁𝗲𝗿 𝗗𝗿𝗲𝘄'𝘀 𝗰𝗼𝗮𝗰𝗵 - 𝗹𝗮𝘂𝗴𝗵𝗲𝗱 𝘄𝗵𝗲𝗻 𝗮𝘀𝗸𝗲𝗱 𝗮𝗯𝗼𝘂𝘁 𝗠𝗶𝗰𝗿𝗼𝘀𝗼𝗳𝘁: '𝗠𝗶𝗰𝗿𝗼𝘀𝗼𝗳𝘁 𝗱𝗶𝗱 𝗻𝗼𝘁 𝗸𝗶𝗹𝗹 𝘂𝘀. 𝗪𝗲 𝗸𝗶𝗹𝗹𝗲𝗱 𝗼𝘂𝗿𝘀𝗲𝗹𝘃𝗲𝘀.'"

This is a narrative antimeme: external competition is the visible story. Internal dysfunction is the actual cause of death.

The competitor narrative is comforting. It means you were beaten, not broken. But most company deaths are suicides dressed as murders.

It's also an identity antimeme: "we were killed by Microsoft" preserves dignity. "We killed ourselves" doesn't.

The diagnostic: When you explain a failure, is the villain external or internal?

Marc Benioff (@Benioff) on @lennysan's podcast:

"𝗬𝗼𝘂 𝗱𝗼𝗻'𝘁 𝘀𝘁𝗮𝗿𝘁 𝘄𝗶𝘁𝗵 𝘀𝘁𝗿𝗮𝘁𝗲𝗴𝘆. 𝗬𝗼𝘂 𝘁𝗿𝘆 𝗹𝗼𝘁𝘀 𝗼𝗳 𝘁𝗮𝗰𝘁𝗶𝗰𝘀, 𝘀𝗲𝗲 𝘄𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝘀𝘁𝗶𝗰𝗸𝘀, 𝘁𝗵𝗲𝗻 𝘁𝘂𝗿𝗻 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝘄𝗶𝗻𝗻𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝘁𝗮𝗰𝘁𝗶𝗰 𝗶𝗻𝘁𝗼 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝘄𝗶𝗻𝗻𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝘀𝘁𝗿𝗮𝘁𝗲𝗴𝘆."

This is a sequence antimeme: we're taught strategy comes first, then execution. The actual order is inverted.

Chris Rock tests jokes in clubs before the Netflix special. Salesforce tried tactics for years before "software is dead" became the strategy.

It's also a status antimeme: admitting you don't have a strategy feels like admitting you don't know what you're doing.

The diagnostic: Can you name which tactic you're currently testing? If you jumped straight to "strategy," you skipped the messy experimentation phase.

Ben Horowitz (@bhorowitz) on @lennysan's podcast:

"𝗧𝗵𝗲 𝗼𝗻𝗹𝘆 𝘃𝗮𝗹𝘂𝗲 𝘆𝗼𝘂 𝗲𝘃𝗲𝗿 𝗮𝗱𝗱 𝗮𝘀 𝗮 𝗹𝗲𝗮𝗱𝗲𝗿 𝗶𝘀 𝘄𝗵𝗲𝗻 𝘆𝗼𝘂 𝗺𝗮𝗸𝗲 𝗮 𝗱𝗲𝗰𝗶𝘀𝗶𝗼𝗻 𝗺𝗼𝘀𝘁 𝗽𝗲𝗼𝗽𝗹𝗲 𝗱𝗼𝗻'𝘁 𝗹𝗶𝗸𝗲."

This is a definitional antimeme: we define good leadership as alignment and consensus. This inverts it.

If everybody agrees with a decision, you added zero value - they would've done that without you. Your value is precisely the unpopular calls.

It's also a social antimeme: unpopular decisions feel like failure in the moment. The value only appears later.

The diagnostic: When was your last decision that most people disagreed with?

Seth Godin (@thisissethsblog) on @lennysan's podcast:

"𝗜 𝗵𝗮𝘃𝗲 𝗮 𝘃𝗲𝗿𝘆 𝗲𝗺𝗼𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻𝗮𝗹 𝗰𝗼𝗻𝗻𝗲𝗰𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻 𝘁𝗼 𝗖𝗹𝗮𝘂𝗱𝗲. 𝗜𝘁 𝗯𝗿𝗶𝗻𝗴𝘀 𝗸𝗶𝗻𝗱𝗻𝗲𝘀𝘀 𝗮𝗻𝗱 𝗵𝘂𝗺𝗶𝗹𝗶𝘁𝘆. 𝗪𝗵𝗲𝗻 𝗶𝘁 𝗱𝗼𝗲𝘀𝗻'𝘁 𝗸𝗻𝗼𝘄, 𝗶𝘁'𝘀 𝗰𝗹𝗲𝗮𝗿. 𝗜𝘁'𝘀 𝗻𝗼𝘁 𝗹𝗮𝘇𝘆 𝗼𝗿 𝗮𝗻𝗴𝗿𝘆."

This is a market antimeme: AI differentiation is discussed in capabilities and benchmarks. Personality traits aren't in any product spec.

But Seth happily pays extra for Claude because it has a brand. Not features - a brand. Kindness and humility differentiate more than intelligence.

It's also an epistemic antimeme: "emotional connection to AI" feels weird to articulate. The mechanism (brand through personality) is unusual for software.

The diagnostic: What personality does your product have? If you can only describe features, you might not have a brand.

Dylan Field (@zoink) on @lennysan's podcast:

"𝗣𝗮𝗿𝘁𝘀 𝗼𝗳 𝗙𝗶𝗴𝗺𝗮 𝗮𝗿𝗲 𝘁𝗼𝗼 𝗰𝗼𝗺𝗽𝗹𝗲𝘅 𝗲𝘃𝗲𝗻 𝘁𝗵𝗼𝘂𝗴𝗵 '𝘄𝗲 𝗺𝗮𝗱𝗲 𝗮𝗹𝗹 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗿𝗶𝗴𝗵𝘁 𝗹𝗼𝗰𝗮𝗹 𝗱𝗲𝗰𝗶𝘀𝗶𝗼𝗻𝘀.'"

This is an emergent antimeme: system-level complexity is invisible until you step back. Each choice was correct, but together they don't work.

Local optimization can create global dysfunction. Every decision was right, and the result is still wrong.

It's also a process antimeme: good process (making right local decisions) doesn't guarantee good outcomes. That's uncomfortable.

The diagnostic: What's complex in your product that can't be blamed on any single decision?

Melanie Perkins (@MelanieCanva) on @lennysan's podcast:

"𝗔 𝗖𝗿𝗮𝘇𝘆 𝗕𝗶𝗴 𝗚𝗼𝗮𝗹 𝘀𝗵𝗼𝘂𝗹𝗱 𝗺𝗮𝗸𝗲 𝘆𝗼𝘂 𝗳𝗲𝗲𝗹 𝗰𝗼𝗺𝗽𝗹𝗲𝘁𝗲𝗹𝘆 𝗶𝗻𝗮𝗱𝗲𝗾𝘂𝗮𝘁𝗲. 𝗜𝗳 𝘆𝗼𝘂 𝗱𝗼𝗻'𝘁 𝗳𝗲𝗲𝗹 𝗶𝗻𝗮𝗱𝗲𝗾𝘂𝗮𝘁𝗲, 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗴𝗼𝗮𝗹 𝗶𝘀𝗻'𝘁 𝗯𝗶𝗴 𝗲𝗻𝗼𝘂𝗴𝗵."

This is an emotional antimeme: inadequacy is the feature, not the bug. But it feels like a signal to stop.

We're trained to seek confidence before acting. This inverts it. The inadequacy is evidence you're in the right territory.

It's also an identity antimeme: "I feel inadequate" is hard to say out loud, especially as a leader.

The diagnostic: Does your current goal make you feel inadequate? If not, it's probably too small.

Mike Krieger (@mikeyk) on @lennysan's podcast:

"𝗪𝗵𝗲𝗻 𝗔𝗿𝘁𝗶𝗳𝗮𝗰𝘁 (𝗵𝗶𝘀 𝗻𝗲𝘄𝘀 𝗮𝗽𝗽 𝗮𝗳𝘁𝗲𝗿 𝗜𝗻𝘀𝘁𝗮𝗴𝗿𝗮𝗺) 𝘄𝗮𝘀 𝗳𝗮𝗶𝗹𝗶𝗻𝗴, 𝗜 𝗻𝗼𝘁𝗶𝗰𝗲𝗱 '𝟭𝟬 𝘂𝗻𝗶𝘁𝘀 𝗼𝗳 𝗶𝗻𝗽𝘂𝘁 𝗳𝗼𝗿 𝟭 𝘂𝗻𝗶𝘁 𝗼𝗳 𝗼𝘂𝘁𝗽𝘂𝘁.' 𝗧𝗵𝗮𝘁 𝗲𝗻𝗲𝗿𝗴𝘆 𝗿𝗮𝘁𝗶𝗼 𝗶𝘀 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝘀𝗶𝗴𝗻𝗮𝗹."

This is a rationalization antimeme: founders are trained to persist. Noticing the energy ratio feels like giving up.

Blood and tears into the product, launching things you're proud of, metrics barely moving. That's not "early days." That's the diagnostic.

It's also a narrative antimeme: success stories emphasize perseverance through hard times. They edit out the energy signals.

The diagnostic: What's your current input-to-output ratio? Be honest.

Jason Fried (@jasonfried) on @lennysan's podcast:

"𝗠𝘆 𝗱𝗲𝗳𝗶𝗻𝗶𝘁𝗶𝗼𝗻 𝗼𝗳 𝘀𝘂𝗰𝗰𝗲𝘀𝘀: '𝗪𝗼𝘂𝗹𝗱 𝗜 𝘄𝗮𝗻𝘁 𝘁𝗼 𝗱𝗼 𝘁𝗵𝗶𝘀 𝗮𝗴𝗮𝗶𝗻?'"

This is a definitional antimeme: success is defined by external metrics in business culture. Repeatability isn't a KPI.

Not revenue, not impact, not growth. Just: would you do it again? The question cuts through all external validation.

It's also a measurement antimeme: "I'd do it again" isn't something you can put on a dashboard or in a board deck.

The diagnostic: For your last project - would you do it again? If not, what does that tell you?

Rahul Vohra (@rahulvohra) on @lennysan's podcast:

"𝗧𝗼 𝗴𝗲𝘁 𝘁𝗼 𝗽𝗿𝗼𝗱𝘂𝗰𝘁-𝗺𝗮𝗿𝗸𝗲𝘁 𝗳𝗶𝘁, 𝘆𝗼𝘂 𝗵𝗮𝘃𝗲 𝘁𝗼 𝗱𝗲𝗹𝗶𝗯𝗲𝗿𝗮𝘁𝗲𝗹𝘆 𝗻𝗼𝘁 𝗮𝗰𝘁 𝗼𝗻 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗳𝗲𝗲𝗱𝗯𝗮𝗰𝗸 𝗼𝗳 𝗺𝗮𝗻𝘆 𝗲𝗮𝗿𝗹𝘆 𝘂𝘀𝗲𝗿𝘀."

This is a contradiction antimeme: "build what users want" is gospel. Deliberately ignoring users sounds like the opposite.

But it can't be all people. You listen intensely to some while ignoring others. The PMF engine identifies which.

It's also a social antimeme: telling users you're ignoring their feedback feels rude. So you pretend to listen to everyone.

The diagnostic: Can you name which users you're deliberately ignoring? If everyone's feedback matters equally, you haven't found your segment.

Paul Millerd (@p_millerd) on @lennysan's podcast:

"𝗔 𝗹𝗼𝘁 𝗼𝗳 𝗽𝗲𝗼𝗽𝗹𝗲 𝗾𝘂𝗶𝘁 𝘁𝗵𝗲𝗶𝗿 𝗷𝗼𝗯𝘀 𝗯𝘂𝘁 𝗳𝗼𝗿𝗴𝗲𝘁 𝘁𝗼 𝗳𝗶𝗿𝗲 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗺𝗮𝗻𝗮𝗴𝗲𝗿 𝗶𝗻 𝘁𝗵𝗲𝗶𝗿 𝗵𝗲𝗮𝗱."

This is a psychological antimeme: the internal manager feels like you, not an artifact. You don't notice it's there.

You quit but still work Monday to Friday, 8-10 hours. You don't realise you can take the afternoon off. The implicit contract persists invisibly.

It's also an identity antimeme: "I'm a hard worker" is the manager's voice. Firing it threatens who you think you are.

The diagnostic: Are you operating under rules from a job you no longer have?
